\noindent
Both Unit 1 assessments are parallel forms: same blueprint, same $100$ points
(Part A $14$, Part B $16$, Part C $40$, Part D $30$), different numbers and

Set 2 (8--14): \textbf{F C G A E B D}. \quad
Multiple choice (15--22): \textbf{B D A C D A C B}.

\vspace{2pt}
\textbf{Why each MC answer is the right one} (the two forms test the same eight
ideas in the same order, each aimed at one lesson's target misconception):
\textbf{15} (1.0) --- the digit reflex: a route number or ZIP code is a label;
a mean of it describes nobody.
\textbf{16} (1.2) --- one parameter, and it did not move; two honest samples
give two honest statistics. That is sampling variability, not a mistake.
\textbf{17} (1.3) --- a group built from one grade is alike inside: a stratum,
not a cluster. ``Chance chose the groups'' does not rescue it.
\textbf{18} (1.4) --- \emph{could that household have been chosen?} Yes, but
silent $\to$ nonresponse; never on the frame $\to$ undercoverage.
\textbf{19} (1.4) --- size buys precision, not correctness: same method, same
direction, same size of error.
\textbf{20} (1.5) --- nobody was assigned, so it is an association; a lurking
variable fits the data as well as the claim does.
\textbf{21} (1.6) --- the placebo gives both groups the idea of being treated,
so only the real treatment differs.
\textbf{22} (1.7) --- \emph{can you hand this out to a person?} No $\to$
unethical --- a reason for a \emph{weaker} verb, never a stronger one.
\end{teachernote}

\begin{teachernote}[Part D --- Rubric (both forms) and What to Watch]
\scriptsize
\textbf{31 --- the two studies ($10$).} (a) $3$: correct labels $1$, the
deciding feature \emph{who decided the groups} $1$, and that \emph{observing}
is how B collected its data, not how it built its groups $1$ (1.7's crux).
(b) $4$: the experiment $1$, plus the argument that random assignment makes the
groups alike at the start so the program is the only difference left $3$.
\emph{Naming the experiment with no reason caps at $2$;} choosing A because its
gap is bigger earns $0$. (c) $3$: the people who chose the program were already
ahead, so self-selection inflated A's gap; in B nobody chose (1.6's crux ---
\emph{the design buys the verb, not the gap}).

\vspace{2pt}
\textbf{32 --- the mail survey ($10$).} (a) $3$: response rate, percent, and
scale-up, with work. (b) $3$: nonresponse bias $1$, the gap in percentage points
$1$, the gap in households $1$. (c) $2$: recompute the same percent $1$, and say
that sample size does not remove bias $1$. (d) $2$: any change that reaches the
people who did not answer --- follow-up calls or in-person contact on a random
sample --- named together with the bias it removes.

\vspace{2pt}
\textbf{33 --- design and critique ($10$).} (a) $4$: technique $1$, justified
from what the context \emph{allows} $3$ --- a full list exists and the grades
are expected to differ, which is the case for stratified; accept simple random
or systematic with a justification that uses the list. (b) $3$: any of the five
methods, with a reason tied to this question. (c) $3$: chance never chose her
advisory $1$, so the near-match is luck and proves nothing about the method $1$
(1.8's crux), and the one claim she may make --- about her own advisory only $1$.

\vspace{2pt}
\textbf{Two errors to watch for.} (i) On \textbf{33(c)}, ``the sample is too
small'' is the wrong reason --- size is not what fails, chance is --- and ``but
it matched'' is the error the item is built to catch; cap either at $1$.
(ii) Across Part D, an answer that names the right term but never states the
\textbf{direction} of the error, or never says \emph{why} the design earns its
verb, takes half credit on that part --- the emphasis the cover warns students
about.
\end{teachernote}
