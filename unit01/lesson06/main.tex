\begin{tcolorbox}[colback=frost, colframe=cerulean, boxrule=0.9pt, arc=2mm,
    left=2mm, right=2mm, top=1.5mm, bottom=1.5mm]
    \textbf{Primary Objective:} Students will explain what makes a study an
    \emph{experiment}, name the experimental units, treatment, and control group
    in a described design, plan an assignment that is completely randomized or
    blocked, explain blinding and the placebo effect, and evaluate a design
    against \emph{comparison, randomization, replication,} and \emph{control} ---
    justifying why an experiment, unlike an observational study, can support a
    cause-and-effect conclusion.
\end{tcolorbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Priority Ideas \& Skills]{goldbox}
\begin{minipage}[t]{0.49\linewidth}\vspace{0pt}
    \textbf{Skills for this lesson}
    \begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.1em, itemsep=2pt, topsep=2pt]
      \item Identify a study as an \textbf{experiment} by asking whether the
            researcher \emph{assigned} the groups. \hfill \textit{PS.DC.3a}
      \item Name the \textbf{experimental units / subjects}, the
            \textbf{treatment}, and the \textbf{control group}.
            \hfill \textit{PS.DC.3a.i, a.iii}
      \item Explain the \textbf{placebo effect} and say who is
            \textbf{blinded} --- single- vs.\ double-blind.
            \hfill \textit{PS.DC.3a.ii}
      \item Distinguish \textbf{completely randomized}, \textbf{randomized
            block}, and \textbf{matched pairs} designs, and allocate units to
            blocks. \hfill \textit{PS.DC.3a.iv}
      \item Evaluate a design against \textbf{comparison, randomization,
            replication, control} and name the principle a flawed plan breaks.
            \hfill \textit{PS.DC.3b; AFDA.DA.2c}
      \item Justify why random assignment licenses a \textbf{causal}
            conclusion that watching does not.
            \hfill \textit{PS.DC.3b; PS.DC.1b}
    \end{itemize}
\end{minipage}
\hfill
\begin{minipage}[t]{0.47\linewidth}\vspace{0pt}
    \textbf{Key Understandings}
    \begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.1em, itemsep=2pt, topsep=2pt]
      \item \emph{Experiments must be well designed to detect a
            cause-and-effect relationship.} The design is what buys the
            conclusion --- not the size of the gap.
      \item In an experiment the researcher \textbf{assigns} the units and
            \textbf{imposes} the treatment. That is the exact thing Lesson 1.5
            said was missing.
      \item \textbf{Random assignment} makes the two groups alike
            \emph{before} the treatment; \textbf{control} keeps them alike
            \emph{during} it. A \textbf{confounding variable} is a 1.5 lurking
            variable that got inside the experiment.
      \item The control group is not wasted --- without it there is nothing to
            compare against, and comparison is principle one.
      \item Blinding does not change who was treated. It removes the part of
            the response that comes from \emph{knowing}.
      \item A smaller gap can be the stronger evidence: self-selection
            inflates an observed gap, and randomization deflates it to the
            truth.
    \end{itemize}
\end{minipage}
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[{Vocabulary, Concepts, \& Theorems}]{greenbox}
\small
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.35}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{} >{\bfseries}p{3.1cm} X @{}}
Experiment & A study in which the researcher assigns the experimental units to
       groups and imposes a treatment on at least one group. \\
Experimental unit & The individual or object a treatment is applied to. When the
       units are people they are called \textbf{subjects}. \\
Treatment & The condition the researcher applies to a group. \\
Control group & The group that does not receive the treatment; it gives the
       baseline the treatment group is compared against. \\
Random assignment & Using chance --- a generator, a coin, drawn slips --- to
       decide which group each unit goes in. \\
Placebo effect & A subject responds to the \emph{idea} of being treated rather
       than to the treatment itself. A \textbf{placebo} is a fake treatment
       built to look real. \\
Blinding & Keeping someone from knowing which group a subject is in.
       \textbf{Single-blind:} the subjects, or the measurers. \textbf{Double-blind:}
       both. \\
Confounding variable & A variable that differs between the groups and also
       affects the response, so the two explanations cannot be told apart. \\
Completely randomized & Every unit is assigned to a treatment purely by chance. \\
Randomized block & Units are first sorted into blocks of similar units, then
       randomized \emph{within} each block. \textbf{Matched pairs} is the case
       where the block is a pair. \\
\end{tabularx}
\end{skillbox}

\boxguard
\begin{skillbox}[Hook]{frost}
    Put both of Riverbend's studies on the board side by side, with no comment.
    \textbf{Last quarter, watching:} Study Center users $65\%$, non-users
    $80\%$. \textbf{This quarter, assigning:} Study Center group $75\%$, control
    group $55\%$. Same school, same Study Center, opposite conclusions.
    \textbf{Ask: which one should the counselor believe?} Take a vote before any
    reasoning --- the split is worth seeing. Then ask the only question that
    matters: \emph{what is different about how the two groups were built?}
    Somebody will say ``she picked this time.'' Correct them once, precisely:
    \emph{chance} picked. That distinction is the whole lesson, and everything
    after it is machinery for making chance's choice trustworthy.
\end{skillbox}

\boxguard
\begin{skillbox}[Lesson]{frost}
\begin{multicols}{2}
\textbf{1. Assign.} The four-plan table is a sorting task and should move fast
--- it is deliberately the same table shape as 1.5's, with the answers flipped.
\textbf{Ask on every row: who decided which group that person is in?} Land the
three names (units/subjects, treatment, control) on the Riverbend design
immediately, while the context is warm. The control group is the one students
undervalue; say out loud that it is not a wasted group.

\textbf{2. The four principles.} This is PS.DC.3b and it carries the most
weight. Give the definitions from the middle column, then have students fill the
Riverbend column --- each answer is one short phrase and every one is already on
the board. Close on \emph{confounding}: it is 1.5's lurking variable, except it
got \emph{inside} the experiment because the two groups were not treated alike.
Control is the principle that keeps it out.

\textbf{3. Blinding.} Keep this to eight minutes. The placebo effect is easy in
this context --- \emph{being picked for a special program} is itself a
treatment. The three-row table escalates cleanly; row 3 is where the quiet study
hall appears, and it is worth naming out loud that the placebo is what lets you
blind the \emph{students}, not just the graders.

\textbf{4. Three designs.} Name all three, then spend the time on the block
allocation table: $80$ splits $40/40$, $40$ splits $20/20$. \textbf{Ask why she
did not simply hope randomization would even it out} --- chance usually does,
but blocking makes it certain, and the variable she fears is exactly the one
that ruined 1.5.

\textbf{5. The payoff.} Two rows, two verdicts. This is the moment the unit has
been building toward since 1.5, so do not rush it. The last fill-in --- because
\emph{chance assigned the groups} --- is the sentence students should be able to
produce on demand for the rest of the year.

\textbf{Guided practice.} Cedar Ridge. The four flawed plans map one-to-one onto
the four principles; do the first with the class and let them finish. The
closing write is the exit ticket in miniature, and this time the answer is
\emph{yes} --- which surprises students who spent 1.5 learning to say no.
\end{multicols}
\end{skillbox}

\boxguard
\begin{skillbox}[Explicit Instruction: Evaluating an Experimental Design]{frost}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{} p{0.46\linewidth} X @{}}
\begin{minipage}[t]{\linewidth}\vspace{0pt}
\textbf{Steps --- give these verbatim}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.4em, itemsep=2pt, topsep=2pt]
  \item \textbf{Did the researcher assign the groups?} Yes $\Rightarrow$
        experiment. No $\Rightarrow$ observational (Lesson 1.5).
  \item \textbf{Name the pieces:} experimental units, treatment, control group,
        response variable.
  \item \textbf{Comparison?} Is there a group that did \emph{not} get the
        treatment?
  \item \textbf{Randomization?} Did \emph{chance} do the assigning --- not the
        researcher's judgment, not the subject's preference, not the sign-up
        order?
  \item \textbf{Replication?} Are there many units per group, or just a few?
  \item \textbf{Control?} Is anything else different between the groups? Name
        it --- that is the confounding variable.
  \item \textbf{Blinding?} Who could be kept from knowing, and who could not?
  \item \textbf{Then write the conclusion:} with all four principles met, a
        causal verb is allowed. With one broken, name which one and stop.
\end{enumerate}
\end{minipage}
&
\begin{minipage}[t]{\linewidth}\vspace{0pt}
\textbf{Worked example --- ``Riverbend takes $120$ volunteers, lets a random
number generator send $60$ to the Study Center twice a week and $60$ nowhere,
and keeps everything else about the quarter the same. $45$ of the $60$ treated
passed every class; $33$ of the $60$ controls did.''}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.1em, itemsep=2pt, topsep=2pt]
  \item[1.] The generator assigned the groups. Experiment.
  \item[2.] Units: the $120$ volunteers. Treatment: attending twice a week.
        Control: the $60$ kept out. Response: passed every class or not.
  \item[3.] Comparison: yes --- $60$ untreated students.
  \item[4.] Randomization: yes --- a generator, not the sign-up sheet.
  \item[5.] Replication: yes --- $60$ per group, not $1$.
  \item[6.] Control: same quarter, same classes, same teachers. Nothing else
        differs.
  \item[7.] Students cannot be blinded here; the graders can be.
  \item[8.] $45/60 = 75\%$ against $33/60 = 55\%$, a $20$-point gap.
        \textit{Attending the Study Center caused a higher pass rate.}
\end{itemize}
\textit{Contrast with 1.5's memo on the same Study Center, which had the arrow
backwards. The data did not get better --- the design did.}
\end{minipage}
\end{tabularx}
\end{skillbox}

\boxguard
\begin{skillbox}[Active Monitoring]{redbox}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em, itemsep=2pt, topsep=2pt]
  \item \textbf{Watch for ``association is not causation'' applied to an
        experiment.} It is Lesson 1.5's tool, and this is the one lesson where
        the causal verb is allowed. Redirect: \emph{who built these groups?}
  \item \textbf{Watch for ``random'' meaning random \emph{selection}.} Choosing
        who is in the study (1.3) and choosing who gets the treatment are two
        different randomizations. Ask which one the sentence is about.
  \item \textbf{Watch for ``the researcher picked'' as an answer to who decided
        the groups.} Half credit --- the researcher ran the generator; chance
        made the choice. The distinction is the lesson.
  \item \textbf{Watch for control confused with control group.} Control is a
        principle (hold everything else equal); the control group is a group.
        A design can have a control group and still fail control.
  \item \textbf{Watch for a confounding variable that is really the treatment.}
        Test: \emph{would it still differ between the groups if nobody had been
        treated?}
  \item \textbf{Cold-call prompts:} ``Who assigned?'' ``What does the control
        group get?'' ``Which principle is missing?'' ``What else is different
        between these two groups?'' ``Say the conclusion, and tell me what
        earns you that verb.''
\end{itemize}
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Group Work \& Differentiation]{redbox}
\small
\begin{multicols}{3}
\textbf{Tier R --- Remediate}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1em, itemsep=1pt, topsep=2pt]
  \item Name the volunteer counts and the two group sizes.
  \item Decide it is an experiment and say that \emph{chance} assigned.
  \item Compute $27 \div 45 = 60\%$ and $18 \div 45 = 40\%$.
  \item Say which group is higher, and by how many points.
\end{itemize}

\columnbreak
\textbf{Tier A --- Approaching Proficiency}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1em, itemsep=1pt, topsep=2pt]
  \item Pool the volunteers: $45/90 = 50\%$, scaled to $750$ of $1{,}500$.
  \item Name units, treatment, and control group.
  \item Say how the design meets all four principles, one phrase each.
  \item Explain why the $20$-point gap beats 1.5's $25$-point gap.
\end{itemize}

\columnbreak
\textbf{Tier E --- Extension}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1em, itemsep=1pt, topsep=2pt]
  \item Block on retirement: $30 \to 15/15$, $60 \to 30/30$.
  \item Name the principle each of two altered plans breaks.
  \item Decide whether the study can be double-blind, and say who cannot be
        blinded.
\end{itemize}
\end{multicols}
\end{skillbox}

\boxguard
\begin{skillbox}[Individual Work \& Assessment]{redbox}
\textbf{Exit ticket (3 items, no notes).} One context: Bayside Middle School,
$80$ sixth-grade volunteers, a $10$-minute silent reading block.
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.4em, itemsep=2pt, topsep=2pt]
  \item Identify it as an experiment, say chance assigned the groups, and name
        the treatment and the control group.
  \item Compute $28 \div 40 = 70\%$ and $16 \div 40 = 40\%$, then the
        $30$-point gap. Equal denominators this time --- a student who still
        divides by $80$ has not noticed that the groups were built.
  \item \textbf{Conceptual / justification check:} a teacher proposes giving
        the reading block to whoever wants it. Full credit names
        \emph{randomization} \emph{and} says what goes wrong --- the students
        who want it may already be the stronger readers. Grade this one first.
\end{enumerate}
\textbf{Using the results:} sort into three piles --- (a) named randomization
and attached a concrete consequence, (b) named a principle with no consequence
attached or named the wrong one, (c) said the change was fine. Pile (c) is the
one to catch: they have not connected self-selection to the arithmetic, and
Lesson 1.7 asks them to weigh the two study types against each other. Open 1.7
by handing pile (c) the Riverbend pair again --- $65\%$ vs.\ $80\%$ against
$75\%$ vs.\ $55\%$ --- and asking which sentence each study earns.
\end{skillbox}

\boxguard
\begin{skillbox}[Reinforcement \& Extension]{goldbox}
\textbf{Homework --- Millbrook Public Library, this time they assign.} The same
Summer Reading Program students studied observationally in Lesson 1.5, now run
as an experiment: $200$ volunteers, a generator sends $100$ to the program and
$100$ to a fall waiting list. $65$ and $35$ read at least $10$ books. Students
identify it as an experiment, compute $65\%$ and $35\%$ and the $30$-point gap,
pool the volunteers ($100/200 = 50\%$, scaled to $600$ of $1{,}200$), name units
/ treatment / control / response, say how the design meets each of the four
principles, match four altered plans to the principle each breaks, allocate a
block design on age ($120 \to 60/60$, $80 \to 40/40$), and close by explaining
why this year's \emph{smaller} $30$-point gap is stronger evidence than last
year's $48$.

\textbf{Extension (optional).} Students take the causal headline they found for
Lesson 1.5's extension and design the experiment that would actually test it ---
units, treatment, control, the exact randomization, one variable held constant,
and who could be blinded. With the 1.2--1.5 extensions this is now a complete
study proposal, which is precisely what Lesson 1.8's project asks for.

\textbf{Preview of Lesson 1.7.} Both study types side by side: what each one
lets you conclude, when an experiment is impossible or unethical to run, and how
to choose a data collection method for a given context (PS.DC.3c--e).

\textbf{Connections \& Big Ideas.} \textit{Unit 1 core idea:} a conclusion is
only as trustworthy as the study that produced it. \textit{Standards covered:}
PS.DC.3a (i--iv), PS.DC.3b; AFDA.DA.2c --- with PS.DC.1b carried forward in
every conclusion item. \textit{Spirals forward to:} comparing experiments with
observational studies and choosing a collection method (1.7, PS.DC.3c--e), the
class study project (1.8), comparing two distributions (2.7), two-way tables
(3.1), and inference, where the whole point of a $p$-value is deciding whether a
treatment gap like $20$ points is bigger than chance alone would give
(7.5--7.6, 8.4--8.5). \textit{Reaches back to:} Lesson 1.3's randomization
machinery, Lesson 1.4's bias, and Lesson 1.5's lurking variable, which reappears
here as a confounding variable the design is built to defeat.
\end{skillbox}

\begin{teachernote}[Warm-Up]
    Five minutes, silent, timed. Every move is one the class has run in 1.2,
    1.4, and 1.5, so this should be near-universal --- and it is easier than
    1.5's warm-up on purpose, because both denominators are $60$.

    Say that out loud at the collection. In Lesson 1.5 the two groups were $60$
    and $90$ because the students sorted themselves; here they are $60$ and $60$
    because a generator split them. Equal group sizes are a \emph{symptom} of
    assignment, and noticing it is worth more than the arithmetic.

    Item 3(b) is the only place to lose anyone: the pooled rate is $78 \div 120
    = 65\%$, not the average of $75$ and $55$. Here the average happens to give
    $65\%$ too, because the groups are equal --- do not let that pass as a
    method. Ask why it worked this time and not in 1.5.

    Leave $75\%$, $55\%$, and $20$ points on the board. The hook needs them
    thirty seconds later, beside 1.5's $65\%$ and $80\%$.
\end{teachernote}

\begin{teachernote}[Guided Notes]
    Pace: section 1 in 8 minutes, section 2 in 12, section 3 in 8, section 4 in
    10, section 5 in 9, guided practice in 8. \textbf{Sections 2 and 5 are the
    lesson.} Section 1 is a definition and a sorting table and should move.

    Section 2 is where PS.DC.3b is met and where students will need the most
    talk time. The middle column is given because it is the standard's own
    language; the work is the right-hand column, and every answer is a short
    phrase already visible on the board. The confounding-variable sentence at
    the bottom is the bridge from 1.5 --- say it as \emph{same variable, new
    name, because it got inside}.

    Section 3 lands best if you make the placebo concrete before the table:
    ask what would happen if the treatment group were told they had been
    ``selected for an academic excellence program.'' Somebody always says they
    would try harder. That \emph{is} the placebo effect, and the quiet study
    hall in row 3 is the fix.

    Section 5 is the payoff of two lessons and deserves its full nine minutes.
    Do not fill in the final blank for them. Wait for \emph{chance assigned the
    groups}; anything vaguer than that is not yet the idea.

    If time runs short, compress section 4 to naming the three designs and the
    block table --- the homework re-teaches blocking with its own allocation.
    Never compress section 5.
\end{teachernote}

\begin{teachernote}[Group Activity]
    Groups of three, 15--18 minutes, tiers assigned rather than chosen. Read the
    headline box aloud, especially the first sentence: this is the experiment
    that finally settles the question 1.5 left open, and groups should feel that
    they are closing a loop rather than starting a new context.

    Tier R is deliberately gentler than 1.5's --- both denominators are $45$.
    The item to watch is 2(b): ``the director'' is half credit, ``chance'' or
    ``the random generator'' is full. Insist on it here, because the exit
    ticket asks the same question.

    Tier A item 3 is the four-principle sweep and is the item most groups
    under-answer. A phrase per principle is enough, but it has to be a
    \emph{Harbor Point} phrase --- ``randomization: the generator split them,''
    not ``randomization: it was random.'' Item 4 is the interpretive item and
    the best answer in the activity: the $25$-point gap was inflated by
    retirement, and random assignment spread the retired members across both
    groups, so $20$ points is the swim time by itself. Pull it to the whole
    class if two groups reach it.

    Tier E item 1's block table is $15/15$ and $30/30$ --- the arithmetic is
    trivial and the follow-up question is the point: chance usually evens
    retirement out, but blocking makes it certain, and retirement is exactly
    the variable that ruined the previous study. Item 3 is the honest limit of
    the design: a volunteer obviously knows whether they swam at $6$~a.m., so
    the subjects cannot be blinded, but the staff scoring the sleep logs can be.
    Accept ``single-blind'' as the label; insist on the reason.
\end{teachernote}

\begin{teachernote}[Exit Ticket]
    Five minutes, notes closed, collected at the door. Items 1 and 2 should be
    near-universal after the activity; if 1(a) is not, section 1 did not land
    and it is worth three minutes at the start of Lesson 1.7.

    Item 1(b) asks for treatment and control group by name. A student who
    writes ``the reading block'' and ``the other students'' has it; a student
    who writes the two group sizes has not answered the question asked.

    Both denominators are $40$, so the arithmetic is a gift --- which is the
    point. A student who divides by $80$ has carried over 1.5's habit of
    hunting for a shared whole and has not registered that the researcher built
    two equal groups.

    Grade item 3 first and only item 3 if time is short. Full credit needs both
    halves: \emph{randomization}, and a consequence tied to \emph{this} context
    --- the students who want the reading block are probably already the
    stronger readers, so their group would improve more even if the block did
    nothing. A bare ``it wouldn't be random'' is half credit; write the missing
    half on the paper.
\end{teachernote}

\boxguard[26]
\begin{teachernote}[Homework]
    Assign all eight items; about $30$ minutes. Items 4, 5, 6, 7(b), and 8
    cannot be answered by computing anything --- flag them when assigning,
    because students who treat this as a calculation sheet will skip exactly
    those.

    Items 2 and 3 are the arithmetic spine: $65\%$, $35\%$, the $30$-point gap,
    then the pooled $50\%$ and $600$. With both groups at $100$ the percents
    read straight off the counts, so any error here is a place-value slip rather
    than a method problem.

    Item 5 wants a phrase per principle, in context. The most common
    under-answer is restating the definition (``replication: there are enough
    people'') instead of citing the design (``$100$ card holders in each
    group''). Item 6's four rows map one-to-one onto the four principles and are
    in the same order as item 5, which makes it self-checking --- point that out
    only if a student is stuck. The last row is the subtle one: living near the
    library is a confounding variable, and the answer is \emph{control}, not
    randomization.

    Item 8 is the closer and the reason this homework reuses 1.5's library. Last
    summer's $48$-point gap was bigger and worth less, because the card holders
    who signed up were already the heavy readers. This summer's $30$ points
    belong to the program alone. Students who answer ``a bigger gap is always
    better evidence'' are reasoning from the number instead of the design ---
    write the correction rather than marking it, because the same reflex will
    cost them on the unit test and again in Unit 7.

    The \textbf{remindbox} hands off to Lesson 1.7 and deliberately raises the
    question of when an experiment is impossible or unethical. Do not resolve it
    here.
\end{teachernote}
