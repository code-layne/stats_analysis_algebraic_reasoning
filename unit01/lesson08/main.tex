    left=2mm, right=2mm, top=1.5mm, bottom=1.5mm]
    \textbf{Primary Objective:} Students will plan and conduct a survey of
    their own through all four stages of the statistical cycle --- writing a
    statistical question, choosing a sample, building a neutral instrument,
    collecting real data from real classmates, organizing the answers into a
    frequency table and a bar graph, and reporting a conclusion in context ---
    and will judge the \emph{validity} of that conclusion by naming whom their
    design actually allows them to describe.
\end{tcolorbox}

    \textbf{Skills for this lesson}
    \begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.1em, itemsep=2pt, topsep=2pt]
      \item Use the \textbf{statistical cycle} to plan and conduct an
            observational study. \hfill \textit{PS.DC.2d}
      \item Build a \textbf{survey instrument}: one idea per item, neutral
            wording, non-overlapping choices. \hfill \textit{AFDA.DA.2d}
      \item \textbf{Collect} real data from a real sample and tally it into a
            frequency table. \hfill \textit{AFDA.DA.2d}
      \item Compute \textbf{relative frequencies} and scale a sample percent
            to a population. \hfill \textit{AFDA.DA.2i}
      \item Select and complete the \textbf{visual representation} that fits
            categorical data. \hfill \textit{AFDA.DA.2h}
      \item \textbf{Communicate} the study, its data, and the
            \textbf{validity} of its conclusion. \hfill \textit{AFDA.DA.2j}
    \end{itemize}
\end{minipage}
\hfill
\begin{minipage}[t]{0.47\linewidth}\vspace{0pt}
    \textbf{Key Understandings}
    \begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.1em, itemsep=2pt, topsep=2pt]
      \item A survey is an \emph{observational study} --- so the whole unit's
            machinery applies to the students' own project, not just to
            somebody else's.
      \item Each stage hands something to the next. A bad question cannot be
            repaired by good arithmetic later.
      \item Bias written into the \emph{instrument} is in the data
            permanently. There is no downstream fix.
      \item A percent may be scaled to a population only when \textbf{chance}
            chose the sample --- which is exactly what a class does not have.
      \item Naming the limit is part of the answer. A report that admits its
            convenience sample is \emph{better} work, not weaker work.
      \item The class study and the council's stratified survey nearly agree.
            Agreement by luck is still not evidence.
    \end{itemize}
\end{minipage}
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[{Vocabulary, Concepts, \& Theorems}]{greenbox}
\small
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.35}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{} >{\bfseries}p{3.1cm} X @{}}
Survey instrument & The actual list of questions, in the order they will be
       asked, that every person in the sample answers. The tool that turns a
       statistical question into data. \\
Closed-response item & A question offering a fixed set of choices, so every
       answer falls into one category and the answers can be counted. \\
Open-response item & A question answered in the person's own words. Rich, but
       it cannot be tallied without extra work. \\
Relative frequency & A category's count divided by the total number of
       responses, written as a decimal or a percent. All of them add to
       $100\%$. \\
Bar graph & A display for categorical data: one bar per category, bars drawn
       separated, height equal to the frequency or relative frequency. \\
Validity of a conclusion & Whether the design actually supports the claim ---
       who was sampled, how they were chosen, and therefore whom the result
       may describe. \\
Convenience sample & A sample chosen because it was available. A class
       surveying itself is one, every time. \\
\end{tabularx}
\end{skillbox}

\boxguard
\begin{skillbox}[Hook]{frost}
    Two council members are arguing about the \$$3{,}000$: one says
    \emph{everybody wants longer lunch}, the other says \emph{nobody I know
    does}. Let the class enjoy it for a moment, then point out that both of
    them are describing the people they happen to sit with --- and that this is
    the same error the class is about to be at risk of. Put the council's real
    numbers up: $35\%$, $30\%$, $25\%$, $10\%$, longer lunch \emph{last}.
    \textbf{Ask what the council has that the two arguing members do not.} The
    one-word answer is \emph{data}; the second half of the question is the
    lesson --- it cost them a plan, a named population, $60$ students chosen by
    chance, and one question asked the same way every time. Today the class
    pays that price itself.
\end{skillbox}

\boxguard
\begin{skillbox}[Lesson]{frost}
\begin{multicols}{2}
\textbf{1. The project on one page.} The cycle table is a checklist, not new
content --- students have filled this shape in 1.5 and 1.7. What is new is the
third column: \emph{what you hand in}. \textbf{Say plainly that a survey is an
observational study} (1.5), so PS.DC.2d's cycle is literally today's to-do
list. Five minutes, no more.

\textbf{2. The question and the sample.} The three tests come from 1.1 and the
stratified allocation from 1.3, so both should be fast. The row that matters is
the last one: \emph{when your class surveys only this room, chance did not
choose them --- the schedule did.} Land the word \textbf{convenience sample}
here and leave it on the board; the activity's Tier E and the exit ticket both
turn on it.

\textbf{3. The instrument.} This is the genuinely new content of the lesson.
Four rules, then three broken items to repair. Students met all three failures
in 1.4 as response bias; make them say the 1.4 name out loud (\emph{leading /
demand}, \emph{double-barrelled}, \emph{overlapping choices}) before writing
the repair. The closing line is the point: a biased instrument is wrong in a
\emph{direction}, and no arithmetic downstream can pull it back.

\textbf{4. Represent, then analyze.} The relative frequency column is four
divisions by $60$; do the first together. The bar graph's axes are drawn and
scaled --- students draw four bars only. \textbf{Insist on the gaps between the
bars} and ask why: the categories are labels, not a number line. That question
returns on the homework.

\textbf{Guided practice.} The four-sentence report frame. Sentence 4 is the
only hard one and it is the one the project is graded on. Then the validity
check: what earns the council the right to talk about all $900$, and what a
noon cafeteria sample would have cost it.
\end{multicols}
\end{skillbox}

\boxguard
\begin{skillbox}[Explicit Instruction: Running the Cycle on Your Own Question]{frost}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{} p{0.46\linewidth} X @{}}
\begin{minipage}[t]{\linewidth}\vspace{0pt}
\textbf{Steps --- give these verbatim}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.4em, itemsep=2pt, topsep=2pt]
  \item \textbf{Write the question} so the answers vary, a group is named, and
        a measurement is named.
  \item \textbf{Name the population} --- the group the answer will be about ---
        and then name the sample you can actually reach.
  \item \textbf{Choose the technique and the method.} If chance cannot choose,
        say so now, not at the end.
  \item \textbf{Write the instrument:} one idea per item, neutral wording,
        choices that cover everyone without overlapping, short.
  \item \textbf{Collect.} Ask everyone the same way. Tally as you go.
  \item \textbf{Represent:} frequency table, then relative frequency, then a
        bar graph.
  \item \textbf{Analyze:} percents, largest and smallest, and the scaled
        estimate \emph{if} chance chose the sample.
  \item \textbf{Report in four sentences}, and name the limit yourself before
        a reader finds it.
\end{enumerate}
\end{minipage}
&
\begin{minipage}[t]{\linewidth}\vspace{0pt}
\textbf{Worked example --- the Student Council's grant survey.}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.1em, itemsep=2pt, topsep=2pt]
  \item[1.] ``Which of four options would the most Riverbend students choose
        for the \$$3{,}000$?''
  \item[2.] Population: all $900$ students. Sample: $60$.
  \item[3.] Stratified at $60 \div 900 = \frac{1}{15}$, so $18$/$16$/$14$/$12$
        by grade; method is a survey.
  \item[4.] One closed-response item, four choices, no overlap.
  \item[5.] All $60$ answered --- a $100\%$ response rate.
  \item[6.] $21$/$18$/$15$/$6$ becomes $35\%$/$30\%$/$25\%$/$10\%$.
  \item[7.] $0.35 \times 900 = 315$ students for club funding.
  \item[8.] ``Club funding is the single choice most Riverbend students
        support --- about $315$ of the $900$.'' Allowed: chance chose the
        sample and every grade is represented.
\end{itemize}
\textit{Now change step 2 to ``the $25$ students in this room'' and step 8
collapses to a sentence about $25$ people. Same arithmetic, a much smaller
claim.}
\end{minipage}
\end{tabularx}
\end{skillbox}

\boxguard
\begin{skillbox}[Active Monitoring]{redbox}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em, itemsep=2pt, topsep=2pt]
  \item \textbf{Watch for a question with no measurement in it.} ``What do
        people think about lunch?'' is a conversation topic. Ask: \emph{what
        exactly will you write down for each person?}
  \item \textbf{Watch for the instrument being written after the data are
        collected.} Some groups will just start asking. Stop them --- the
        wording has to be fixed before the first person answers, or the
        answers are not comparable.
  \item \textbf{Watch for overlapping choices.} It is the least obvious of the
        four rules and the one that breaks the $100\%$ check. If the percents
        do not add to $100$, look at the choices before you look at the
        arithmetic.
  \item \textbf{Watch for a scaled estimate on a class sample.} A group that
        writes ``so $324$ of the $900$ students want club funding'' has done
        the arithmetic right and the statistics wrong. This is the error to
        pull to the whole class.
  \item \textbf{Watch for ``our sample is bad'' as an apology.} Naming the
        limit is the skill. Push them to say it as a sentence with a
        \emph{whom} in it, not as a shrug.
  \item \textbf{Cold-call prompts:} ``What will you write down for each
        person?'' ``Who is your population?'' ``Who chose this sample?''
        ``Do your percents add to $100$?'' ``About whom is that sentence?''
\end{itemize}
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Group Work \& Differentiation]{redbox}
\small
\begin{multicols}{3}
\textbf{Tier R --- Remediate}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1em, itemsep=1pt, topsep=2pt]
  \item Name the population and count the sample.
  \item Fill the four cycle stages for the class question.
  \item Name the collection method and give one reason.
\end{itemize}

\columnbreak
\textbf{Tier A --- Approaching Proficiency}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1em, itemsep=1pt, topsep=2pt]
  \item Write the item and four non-overlapping choices.
  \item Ask everyone; tally; fill count and percent columns.
  \item Compute largest and smallest as percents.
  \item Check that the four percents total $100\%$.
\end{itemize}

\columnbreak
\textbf{Tier E --- Extension}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1em, itemsep=1pt, topsep=2pt]
  \item Scale the top percent to $900$ --- then refuse to report it.
  \item Write the honest sentence, with its \emph{whom}.
  \item Explain why near-agreement with the council is luck.
  \item Name one fix and what it costs.
\end{itemize}
\end{multicols}
\end{skillbox}

\boxguard
\begin{skillbox}[Individual Work \& Assessment]{redbox}
\textbf{Exit ticket (3 items, no notes).} One context: a Bayside homeroom of
$30$ that surveyed itself and reported to a school of $750$.
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.4em, itemsep=2pt, topsep=2pt]
  \item Compute $12 \div 30 = 40\%$, then $0.40 \times 750 = 300$. Both are
        gifts, and the second one is a trap they are meant to walk into ---
        the arithmetic is right and the reporting will not be.
  \item ``Theme'' is categorical, so a bar graph; the tallest bar's height is
        the \emph{share} that chose decades, not the number of students.
  \item \textbf{Conceptual / justification check:} the flawed report sentence.
        Full credit names the convenience sample \emph{and} rewrites the claim
        so it describes the $30$ students only. Grade this one first.
\end{enumerate}
\textbf{Using the results:} sort into three piles --- (a) rewrote the claim and
named the sample, (b) knew something was wrong but rewrote it as ``most
students probably want decades,'' (c) accepted the sentence. Pile (b) is the
useful one: they have the instinct and not the vocabulary, and one sentence of
feedback fixes it. Pile (c) needs the convenience-sample idea re-taught before
the unit test, where it is worth several points across Parts C and D.
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Reinforcement \& Extension]{goldbox}
\textbf{Homework --- Millbrook Public Library, the phone survey and the
clipboard.} The library that was watched in 1.5, assigned in 1.6, and studied a
third time in 1.7 now runs a survey: an SRS of $80$ card holders reached by
phone, $28$/$24$/$20$/$8$ across four programs. Students complete the relative
frequency column ($35\%$/$30\%$/$25\%$/$10\%$), scale two of them to the
$1{,}200$ card holders ($420$ and $300$), read a bar graph and say why the bars
have gaps, repair two broken instrument items, fill the four stages of the
cycle, then compare against a front-desk clipboard where $30$ of $50$ chose
story hour --- $60\%$ against $25\%$, a $35$-point gap worth about $420$ card
holders --- name the bias and its direction, compute a $40\%$ response rate,
and write the sentence the phone survey earns.

\textbf{Extension (optional) --- the project deliverable.} Students finish the
four-sentence report on the data their own class collected today, then add one
sentence naming the limit they would fix first and what fixing it would cost.
This is the piece to collect and grade as the project.

\textbf{Preview.} Unit 1 is finished. Next class is review, then the unit test;
Unit 2 opens by asking what a distribution of a single \emph{quantitative}
variable looks like, where a bar graph is replaced by a dot plot and a
histogram (PS.DS.1a).

\textbf{Connections \& Big Ideas.} \textit{Unit 1 core idea:} a conclusion is
only as trustworthy as the study that produced it --- and today the study is
theirs. \textit{Standards covered:} AFDA.DA.2d, 2h, 2i, 2j; PS.DC.2d --- with
AFDA.DA.2a--c and 2e--g exercised throughout the planning. \textit{Spirals
forward to:} displays of quantitative data (2.1--2.2), comparing distributions
(2.7), categorical displays and two-way tables (3.1), the data cycle with
bivariate data (5.7), and inference, where a sample percent finally acquires a
margin of error (7.2--7.4). \textit{Reaches back to:} every lesson in the unit
--- 1.1's cycle and data types, 1.2's population and sample, 1.3's stratified
allocation, 1.4's bias, 1.5's observational study, and 1.7's collection
methods.
\end{skillbox}

\begin{teachernote}[Warm-Up]
    Five minutes, silent, timed. Nothing is new: two divisions by $60$ and one
    multiplication, all of which the class has run since 1.2.

    Item 3(b) is the only item that is not arithmetic, and it is the one to
    collect aloud. The four percents add to $100\%$ because every student
    picked exactly one choice and the four options left nobody out. That is
    instrument rule 3 arriving forty minutes early --- say so when you collect
    it, and the notes' rule list will land as a name for something they have
    already noticed.

    Leave $35\%$, $30\%$, $25\%$, $10\%$, and $315$ on the board and do not
    erase them. The hook needs them immediately, the notes' section 4 rebuilds
    them, the activity compares against them, and the slides show them twice.
\end{teachernote}

\begin{teachernote}[Guided Notes]
    Pace: section 1 in 5 minutes, section 2 in 10, section 3 in 12, section 4
    in 10, guided practice in 8. \textbf{Section 3 is the new content} --- the
    other three are the unit's own material reassembled as a checklist, and
    they should move fast.

    Section 1's third column is the only column that is blank. Read the whole
    table aloud once before anyone writes; students who see all four
    deliverables at the start plan better in the activity.

    Section 2's stratified arithmetic is 1.3's, unchanged. The item that
    matters is the last sentence of the box, and it is about them: the class is
    a convenience sample because the schedule chose it. Expect resistance ---
    somebody will argue that their class is ``pretty representative.'' The
    answer is not that it is unrepresentative; it is that nobody can know,
    because chance was never involved. Write \textbf{convenience sample} on the
    board and leave it there.

    Section 3 is the lesson. Do not shorten it. For each broken item, ask for
    the 1.4 name before the repair --- leading, double-barrelled, overlapping.
    The age item is the one students accept as fine: point at $16$ and ask
    which box a $16$-year-old checks.

    Section 4: do the first division together, then let them finish. The bar
    graph is drawing four bars on axes that are already scaled --- two minutes,
    not ten. Ask why the bars have gaps; it returns as homework item 3(b).

    If time runs short, compress section 1 to reading the table aloud. Never
    compress section 3 or the guided practice's validity question.
\end{teachernote}

\begin{teachernote}[Group Activity]
    \textbf{This is the lesson's deliverable and it needs the room.} Budget
    20--25 minutes and settle the class question \emph{before} groups split up
    --- one question, asked by everyone, or the tallies cannot be combined. The
    grant question in the key is a safe default if the class stalls; anything
    with four clean, non-overlapping choices works.

    \textbf{The key is a worked exemplar, not the only right answer.} It runs
    on a class of $25$ answering the grant question $9$/$7$/$6$/$3$, which
    gives $36\%$/$28\%$/$24\%$/$12\%$ and scales to $324$. Your class's numbers
    will differ; the arithmetic in the \texttt{work} blocks is there so
    students have the shape of the computation, and you should mark against
    their own totals. What is \emph{not} negotiable is the structure: four
    percents that add to $100\%$, and a sentence in Tier E item 2 whose subject
    is this class and not the school.

    Tier R is planning and should take five minutes. Groups that cannot name a
    population are the ones to sit with --- everything downstream depends on
    it.

    Tier A is where the period can disappear. Give a hard two-minute limit on
    writing the item, then send them to collect. Check every instrument against
    rule 3 before it is used; an overlapping choice set is discovered later as
    percents that do not total $100$, and by then the data are gone.

    Tier E is the payoff and the reason the exemplar's $36\%$ sits so close to
    the council's $35\%$. Item 3(b) is the hardest question in the lesson: the
    two studies nearly agree, and that agreement is \emph{luck}, because chance
    never chose this class. A group that says ``we were close, so our method
    was fine'' has inverted the entire unit --- pull that one to the whole
    class if it appears.
\end{teachernote}

\boxguard[22]
\begin{teachernote}[Exit Ticket]
    Five minutes, notes closed, collected at the door. Items 1(a) and 1(b) are
    deliberately easy and deliberately misleading: $300$ of $750$ is correct
    arithmetic on a sample that has no business speaking for $750$ people.
    Nobody is penalized for computing it --- item 3 is where the reporting is
    judged.

    Item 2(a) should be near-universal. In 2(b), ``$12$ students'' is half
    credit and ``$40\%$ of the homeroom'' is full: the bar's height is a
    \emph{share}, which is exactly what makes it comparable to another
    homeroom of a different size.

    Item 3(a) is the one that separates the class, and it has two halves. The
    flaw is that a single homeroom is a convenience sample; the rewrite must
    change the \emph{subject} of the sentence to the $30$ students. A student
    who names the flaw but leaves the claim about ``Bayside students'' has done
    half the item. Item 3(b) wants the sample changed, not the wording ---
    choose the students by chance from all $750$.

    Grade item 3 first and only item 3 if time is short. It is the single best
    predictor of how the unit test's Part D will go.
\end{teachernote}

\boxguard[26]
\begin{teachernote}[Homework]
    Assign all seven items; about $30$ minutes. The scenario box carries two
    studies of the same library and students must keep them apart --- the phone
    survey is the good one, the clipboard is the trap.

    Items 1 and 2 are the arithmetic spine: $35\%$/$30\%$/$25\%$/$10\%$, then
    $420$ and $300$. All four denominators are $80$, so an error here is a
    place-value slip.

    Item 3(b) is the one most students guess at. The bars are separated because
    the categories are labels, not positions on a number line --- there is no
    ``between book club and coding lab.'' It is worth saying that this is
    exactly what will change in Unit 2, where the variable is a number and the
    bars will touch.

    Item 4 repeats notes section 3 with two of the same three failures. Item 5
    is the cycle table; a \emph{Collect} row that says ``she called people''
    has missed that the sampling lives there.

    Item 6 is the payoff. $30/50 = 60\%$ against the phone survey's $25\%$ is a
    $35$-point gap, which scales to about $420$ card holders --- the same
    number as item 2(a), which is worth pointing out: the bias is as large as
    the entire top result. Full credit on 6(c) names undercoverage or
    self-selection \emph{and} gives the direction (too high).

    Item 7(a) is a $40\%$ response rate, a callback to 1.4's nonresponse. In
    7(b) the sentence must have $1{,}200$ card holders as its subject for the
    phone survey and nobody but the signers for the clipboard.

    \textbf{The extension box is the project deliverable} --- collect and grade
    it. Four sentences plus a named limit. The most common failure is sentence
    4 written about the school rather than the class; that is the same error as
    exit ticket item 3, and seeing it twice in one night is the point.
\end{teachernote}
