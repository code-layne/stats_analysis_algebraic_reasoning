    \textbf{Primary Objective:} Students will explain that data are numbers
    \emph{with a context}, identify the individuals and variables in a data set,
    classify each variable as categorical or quantitative, and decide whether a
    question is a statistical question --- justifying every choice by the context
    it came from.
\end{tcolorbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Priority Ideas \& Skills]{goldbox}
\begin{minipage}[t]{0.49\linewidth}\vspace{0pt}
    \textbf{Skills for this lesson}
    \begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.1em, itemsep=2pt, topsep=2pt]
      \item Read a data table and name the \textbf{individuals} (the rows) and the
            \textbf{variables} (the columns). \hfill \textit{PS.DC.1c; AFDA.DA.2a}
      \item Classify a variable as \textbf{categorical} or \textbf{quantitative},
            and justify the call. \hfill \textit{PS.DC.1c; AFDA.DA.2a}
      \item Explain why a number means nothing without its context ---
            who was measured, what was measured, and in what units.
            \hfill \textit{PS.DC.1a--b}
      \item Recognize and write a \textbf{statistical question} --- one whose
            answers are expected to vary. \hfill \textit{PS.DC.1b; AFDA.DA.2a}
      \item Preview the four stages of the \textbf{data cycle} that organize the
            whole unit. \hfill \textit{PS.DC.1a}
    \end{itemize}
\end{minipage}
\hfill
\begin{minipage}[t]{0.47\linewidth}\vspace{0pt}
    \textbf{Key Understandings}
    \begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.1em, itemsep=2pt, topsep=2pt]
      \item \emph{Data are collected for a purpose and have meaning in a context.}
            ``18'' answers nothing; ``the mean commute was 18 minutes for the six
            students surveyed'' answers something.
      \item The \textbf{type} of a variable decides what you are allowed to do with
            it. You may average a quantitative variable; a categorical variable can
            only be counted and turned into a percent.
      \item \textbf{Numbers can be labels.} Grade level, jersey number, and ZIP code
            are categorical even though they are digits. The test is not ``does it
            look like a number?'' but ``does arithmetic on it mean anything?''
      \item A \textbf{statistical question anticipates variability}. ``How tall am
            I?'' has one answer; ``How tall are students at this school?'' has many.
      \item Every stage of the cycle can go wrong. The rest of Unit 1 is about
            doing each stage well enough to trust the conclusion.
    \end{itemize}
\end{minipage}
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[{Vocabulary, Concepts, \& Theorems}]{greenbox}
\small
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.35}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{} >{\bfseries}p{3.1cm} X @{}}
Data & Numbers or labels collected about individuals, together with the context
       that gives them meaning. \\
Individual & One person, animal, or object that data are collected about ---
       one \emph{row} of a data table. \\
Variable & A characteristic recorded for each individual --- one \emph{column}
       of a data table. \\
Categorical variable & A variable that records a \emph{label} or group name
       (lunch choice, grade level, yes/no). Summarize with counts and percents. \\
Quantitative variable & A variable that records a \emph{measured amount} with
       units (minutes, siblings, dollars). Summarize with a mean or median. \\
Statistical question & A question whose answers are expected to \emph{vary} from
       individual to individual, so it takes data to answer. \\
Population & The entire group you want to know about. \\
Sample & The part of the population you actually collect data from. \\
Data cycle & The four repeating stages of a study: \emph{formulate questions
       $\rightarrow$ collect data $\rightarrow$ organize and represent
       $\rightarrow$ analyze and communicate}. \\
\end{tabularx}
\end{skillbox}

\boxguard
\begin{skillbox}[Hook]{frost}
    Put this on the board before anyone sits down:
    \begin{center}
      \textit{``Four out of five students at Riverbend High School
      say the lunch line is too slow.''}
    \end{center}
    \textbf{Ask:} ``Do you believe it?'' Take votes both ways, then push:
    \textbf{Who did they ask? How many? What exactly did they ask?} Students will
    generate the objections themselves --- \emph{maybe they only asked people
    standing in the line}; \emph{maybe five people}; \emph{maybe they asked ``Isn't
    the lunch line too slow?''} Name what just happened: they did not argue about
    arithmetic, they argued about \textbf{where the number came from}. That is
    Unit 1. Close the hook by writing the four stages of the data cycle on the
    board and telling them each of their objections attacks a different stage.
\end{skillbox}

\boxguard
\begin{skillbox}[Lesson]{frost}
\begin{multicols}{2}
\textbf{1. Data are numbers with a context.} Write a bare ``18'' on the board and
ask what it tells them. Nothing. Now write \emph{``18 minutes --- the mean
one-way commute for the six Riverbend students we surveyed.''} \textbf{Ask: what
three things did I have to add?} \emph{Who} was measured, \emph{what} was
measured, and the \emph{units}. Give the rule: a number without a context is not
data.

\textbf{2. Rows and columns have names.} Project the Riverbend Student Life
Survey table (six students; grade level, commute time, lunch choice, siblings,
club member). \textbf{Ask: what is one row? what is one column?} Name them:
each row is an \textbf{individual}, each column is a \textbf{variable}. Have
students count both --- six individuals, five variables --- before any
classification starts.

\textbf{3. Two types of variables.} Take the commute column and the lunch column.
\textbf{Ask: which one could I find an average of?} Commute, yes; lunch choice,
no --- there is no such thing as an average of ``cafeteria, home, off campus.''
That contrast \emph{is} the definition. Quantitative variables record measured
amounts with units and get a mean; categorical variables record labels and get
counts and percents.

\textbf{4. The trap: numbers that are really labels.} Point at the grade-level
column: 10, 11, 9, 12, 10, 11. \textbf{Ask: it is made of numbers --- is it
quantitative?} Let them argue, then settle it by computing the ``mean grade
level'' aloud: $10.5$. \textbf{Ask: who is in grade 10.5?} Nobody. Grade level is
a \textbf{label written with digits}, so it is categorical. Give them the test to
write down: \emph{does arithmetic on this variable mean anything?} Add jersey
numbers and ZIP codes as the other classic cases.

\textbf{5. Statistical questions.} Ask ``How tall am I?'' --- one answer, measure
once, done. Ask ``How tall are students at Riverbend?'' --- the answers vary from
student to student, so it takes data. Give the definition: a \textbf{statistical
question anticipates variability} in the answers. Run four quick questions and
have the class thumbs-up/thumbs-down each one.

\textbf{6. The road map.} Return to the lunch-line claim from the hook and lay the
four stages of the data cycle over it. Formulate the question; collect the data;
organize and represent it; analyze and communicate. Tell them where the unit
goes: Lessons 1.1--1.2 sharpen the question and the group it describes,
1.3--1.4 attack \emph{how} the data get collected, 1.5--1.7 handle studies and
experiments, and 1.8 has them run the whole cycle themselves.
\end{multicols}
\end{skillbox}

\boxguard
\begin{skillbox}[Explicit Instruction: Classifying a Variable]{frost}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{} p{0.46\linewidth} X @{}}
\begin{minipage}[t]{\linewidth}\vspace{0pt}
\textbf{Steps --- give these verbatim}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.4em, itemsep=2pt, topsep=2pt]
  \item Say the variable out loud in a sentence about \emph{one} individual:
        ``This student's \underline{\hspace{1.6cm}} is \underline{\hspace{1.6cm}}.''
  \item Ask: is the answer a \textbf{label} or a \textbf{measured amount with
        units}?
  \item If it is an amount, ask the check question: \textbf{would averaging these
        mean anything?}
  \item Label: only if the answer is a real amount and averaging makes sense is
        it \textbf{quantitative}. Otherwise it is \textbf{categorical}.
  \item Say how you would summarize it --- mean for quantitative, counts and
        percents for categorical.
\end{enumerate}
\end{minipage}
&
\begin{minipage}[t]{\linewidth}\vspace{0pt}
\textbf{Worked example --- ``Grade level''}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.1em, itemsep=2pt, topsep=2pt]
  \item[1.] ``This student's grade level is 10.''
  \item[2.] It \emph{looks} like an amount --- 10 is a number.
  \item[3.] Mean of $10, 11, 9, 12, 10, 11$ is $10.5$. There is no grade 10.5,
        and nobody is ``half a grade taller.''
  \item[4.] Averaging is meaningless $\Rightarrow$ \textbf{categorical}.
  \item[5.] Summarize by counting: 2 sophomores, 2 juniors, 1 freshman,
        1 senior.
\end{itemize}
\textit{Contrast with ``commute time'': ``this student's commute is 20 minutes,''
a real amount in real units, and 18 minutes is a meaningful average.
$\Rightarrow$ quantitative.}
\end{minipage}
\end{tabularx}
\end{skillbox}

\boxguard
\begin{skillbox}[Active Monitoring]{redbox}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em, itemsep=2pt, topsep=2pt]
  \item \textbf{Watch for the digit reflex.} The single most common error today is
        classifying anything written with digits as quantitative. Circulate
        specifically to the grade-level and jersey-number items and ask,
        \emph{``what would the average of these be, and who would that describe?''}
  \item \textbf{Watch for context-free answers.} A student who writes ``18'' and
        stops has not finished. Require units and the group: ``18 minutes, for
        the six students surveyed.''
  \item \textbf{Watch for ``statistical means it uses numbers.''} Students will
        call ``How many students go to Riverbend?'' statistical because it has a
        number in it. Redirect to variability: \emph{do different people give
        different answers?}
  \item \textbf{Cold-call prompts:} ``Name one individual in this table.''
        ``Name one variable.'' ``Give me a variable made of digits that is
        \emph{not} quantitative.'' ``Change this question so it becomes
        statistical.''
\end{itemize}
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Group Work \& Differentiation]{redbox}
\small
\begin{multicols}{3}
\textbf{Tier R --- Remediate}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1em, itemsep=1pt, topsep=2pt]
  \item Athletics table given; count and name individuals and variables.
  \item Classify three variables with the label/amount question supplied as a
        prompt.
  \item Every answer is read directly from the table --- no scaling, no percents.
\end{itemize}

\columnbreak
\textbf{Tier A --- Approaching Proficiency}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1em, itemsep=1pt, topsep=2pt]
  \item Three short scenarios; for each, name the population, the variable, and
        its type.
  \item One percent computation from a sample, then scaled to the population.
  \item First ``is this question statistical?'' item with a written reason.
\end{itemize}

\columnbreak
\textbf{Tier E --- Extension}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1em, itemsep=1pt, topsep=2pt]
  \item Critique a claim built on a categorical variable that was averaged.
  \item Write an original statistical question, name the variable and its type,
        and say what data would answer it.
  \item Justify why the same recorded value can be categorical in one study and
        quantitative in another.
\end{itemize}
\end{multicols}
\end{skillbox}

\boxguard
\begin{skillbox}[Individual Work \& Assessment]{redbox}
\textbf{Exit ticket (3 items, no notes).}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.4em, itemsep=2pt, topsep=2pt]
  \item Classify two variables from a short context and justify each with the
        averaging test.
  \item Decide which of two questions is statistical, and say why.
  \item \textbf{Conceptual / justification check:} ``The mean of the numbers on
        the team's jerseys is 42.'' What does that tell you about the team?
        Correct answer: \emph{nothing} --- jersey number is a label, so its mean
        describes no real quantity. This is the item to grade first.
\end{enumerate}
\textbf{Using the results:} sort into three piles --- (a) classified correctly
\emph{and} justified, (b) classified correctly but justified by ``it's a number,''
(c) missed the classification. Pile (b) is the biggest and the most fixable;
open Lesson 1.1 with two of their own item-3 responses read aloud, unattributed.
\end{skillbox}

\boxguard
\begin{skillbox}[Reinforcement \& Extension]{goldbox}
\textbf{Homework --- Northgate Community Recreation Center.} A fresh context with
the same spine: identify individuals and variables in a members table, classify
four variables, compute and \emph{interpret} a mean of a quantitative variable,
turn a categorical variable into a percent, scale a sample percent up to the
full membership, and sort four questions into statistical and not. Closes with an
interpretation item that cannot be answered by computing anything.

\textbf{Extension (optional).} Students bring in one real claim --- a headline,
an ad, a social-media poll --- and name the population it claims to describe, the
variable behind it, and that variable's type. Collect these; they seed the bias
discussion in Lesson 1.4.

\textbf{Preview of Lesson 1.1.} We name the four stages of the data cycle
formally, write questions that a data set can actually answer, and sort variables
by type at speed. Today's vocabulary --- individual, variable, categorical,
quantitative --- is assumed from here on.

\textbf{Connections \& Big Ideas.} \textit{Unit 1 core idea:} a conclusion is only
as trustworthy as the study that produced it. \textit{Standards covered:}
PS.DC.1a, PS.DC.1b, PS.DC.1c; AFDA.DA.2a. \textit{Spirals forward to:} population
vs.\ sample and parameter vs.\ statistic (1.2, PS.DC.1d--e), sampling technique
(1.3), and bias (1.4). \textit{Reaches back to:} Algebra 1 percent and
proportional reasoning.
\end{skillbox}

\begin{teachernote}[Warm-Up]
    Five minutes, silent, timed. All three items are Algebra 1 review and should
    be quick; the point is diagnostic, not instructional. Item 2 (scaling
    $18/50$ up to 900 students) is the one that separates the class --- students
    who multiply $18 \times 18$ or add rather than scale the \emph{proportion}
    need a five-minute pull-aside during the activity. Item 3 asks for the mean
    \emph{and} a sentence naming what it describes; do not accept the bare
    number, because the whole lesson turns on that sentence.
\end{teachernote}

\begin{teachernote}[Guided Notes]
    Pace: vocabulary and sections 1--2 in the first 12 minutes, section 3 (the
    two types) in 8, section 4 (numbers that are really labels) in 8. Do
    \emph{not} shortchange section 4 --- it is the misconception that survives
    the whole unit if it is not confronted here, and the ``mean grade level is
    $10.5$'' computation done aloud is what breaks it. Section 5 is fast.
    Section 6 (the road map) is a two-minute preview, not a lesson; resist
    teaching the cycle today, since Lesson 1.1 does it properly.

    In Guided Practice part (b), students will want to answer ``because it's
    words.'' Push once for the real reason: there is no order and no unit, so
    addition is undefined --- and a mean is built from addition.
\end{teachernote}

\begin{teachernote}[Group Activity]
    Groups of three, 15 minutes, tiers assigned rather than chosen. Tier R is a
    reading task and should finish early --- have those groups then attempt Tier
    A's item 1. Tier E item 3 (the same value being categorical in one study and
    quantitative in another) is genuinely hard; accept any defended answer that
    turns on \emph{what was asked}, e.g.\ recording ``how many siblings'' (an
    amount) versus recording ``only child / has siblings'' (a label).

    Circulate to Tier A item 2 first --- it is the only place in the packet where
    the warm-up's scaling skill is reused, and it is where you will find out
    whether the warm-up stuck.
\end{teachernote}

\begin{teachernote}[Exit Ticket]
    Five minutes, notes closed, collected at the door. Grade item 3 first and
    only item 3 if time is short: it is the single item that distinguishes
    students who learned the rule from students who learned the reason. Any
    answer computing something about 42 is a miss, however well written.
    Expect roughly a third of the class to miss it on day one --- that is the
    normal starting point, not a failure of the lesson.
\end{teachernote}

\begin{teachernote}[Homework]
    Assign all seven items; they take about 20 minutes. Item 3 asks for a mean
    and a sentence interpreting it, and item 7 cannot be answered by computing
    anything --- flag both when assigning, because students who treat this as a
    calculation sheet will skip exactly those. The extension (bring in a real
    claim) is worth collecting even from students who skip it as optional;
    those artifacts are reused in Lesson 1.4 for bias.
\end{teachernote}
