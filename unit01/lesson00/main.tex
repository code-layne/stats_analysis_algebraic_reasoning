\begin{tcolorbox}[colback=frost, colframe=cerulean, boxrule=0.9pt, arc=2mm,
    left=2mm, right=2mm, top=1.5mm, bottom=1.5mm]
    \textbf{Primary Objective:} Students will explain that data are numbers \emph{with a
    context}, identify the individuals and variables in a data set, classify each variable
    as categorical or quantitative, and decide whether a question takes data to answer ---
    justifying every choice from the data it came from.
    \\[2pt]
    \textbf{Standards:} PS.DC.1a, PS.DC.1b, PS.DC.1c; AFDA.DA.2a. \emph{Previews:} PS.DC.1d--e
    (1.2), PS.DC.2 (1.3), PS.DC.3 (1.5--1.7).
    \\[2pt]
    \textbf{Lesson model:} \emph{Experience first, formalize later.} Students work the activity
    in small groups using only prior knowledge; the teacher circulates with questions, cues, and
    prompts (not answers); the debrief attaches the formal vocabulary to what the groups already
    found; the application uses it once together, in context. No separate direct-instruction
    block, no tiered instruction, no exit ticket.
\end{tcolorbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Priority Ideas \& Skills]{goldbox}
\begin{minipage}[t]{0.49\linewidth}\vspace{0pt}
    \textbf{Skills for this lesson}
    \begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.1em, itemsep=2pt, topsep=2pt]
      \item Read a data table and name the \textbf{individuals} (the rows) and the
            \textbf{variables} (the columns). \hfill \textit{PS.DC.1c; AFDA.DA.2a}
      \item Classify a variable as \textbf{categorical} or \textbf{quantitative},
            and justify the call. \hfill \textit{PS.DC.1c; AFDA.DA.2a}
      \item Explain why a number means nothing without its context ---
            who was measured, what was measured, and in what units.
            \hfill \textit{PS.DC.1a--b}
      \item Recognize and write a \textbf{statistical question} --- one whose
            answers are expected to vary. \hfill \textit{PS.DC.1b; AFDA.DA.2a}
      \item Preview the four stages of the \textbf{data cycle} that organize the
            whole unit. \hfill \textit{PS.DC.1a}
    \end{itemize}
\end{minipage}
\hfill
\begin{minipage}[t]{0.47\linewidth}\vspace{0pt}
    \textbf{Key Understandings}
    \begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.1em, itemsep=2pt, topsep=2pt]
      \item \emph{Data are collected for a purpose and have meaning in a context.}
            ``18'' answers nothing; ``the mean commute was 18 minutes for the six
            students surveyed'' answers something.
      \item The \textbf{type} of a variable decides what you are allowed to do with
            it. You may average a quantitative variable; a categorical variable can
            only be counted and turned into a percent.
      \item \textbf{Target misconception: the digit reflex.} Students classify
            anything written with digits as quantitative. Jersey number, grade level,
            and ZIP code are categorical. The test is not ``does it look like a
            number?'' but ``does arithmetic on it mean anything?''
      \item A \textbf{statistical question anticipates variability}. ``How tall am
            I?'' has one answer; ``How tall are students at this school?'' has many.
      \item Every stage of the cycle can go wrong. The rest of Unit 1 is about
            doing each stage well enough to trust the conclusion.
    \end{itemize}
\end{minipage}
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[{Vocabulary, Concepts, \& Theorems}]{greenbox}
\small
% Attached in the DEBRIEF. None of these words appears on the cover, in the warm-up, in the
% Activity, or on any pre-debrief slide (the spoiler rule).
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.35}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{} >{\bfseries}p{3.1cm} X @{}}
Data & Numbers or labels collected about individuals, together with the context
       that gives them meaning. \\
Individual & One person, animal, or object that data are collected about ---
       one \emph{row} of a data table. \\
Variable & A characteristic recorded for each individual --- one \emph{column}
       of a data table. \\
Categorical variable & A variable that records a \emph{label} or group name
       (position, lunch choice, grade level, yes/no). Summarize with counts and percents. \\
Quantitative variable & A variable that records a \emph{measured amount} with
       units (points, minutes, siblings). Summarize with a mean or median. \\
Statistical question & A question whose answers are expected to \emph{vary} from
       individual to individual, so it takes data to answer. \\
Population & The entire group you want to know about. \\
Sample & The part of the population you actually collect data from. \\
Data cycle & The four repeating stages of a study: \emph{formulate questions
       $\rightarrow$ collect data $\rightarrow$ organize and represent
       $\rightarrow$ analyze and communicate}. \\
\end{tabularx}
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Experience \& Formalize --- The Activity: \emph{Data Detectives}]{frost}
\textbf{Launch (2 min).} Put the roster on the board and read the blog quote aloud: \emph{``The
Riverbend roster's mean jersey number is 14 --- this is a young, inexperienced team.''} Then
stop. Do not evaluate it. Charge them: \emph{``Two scenarios. Use what you already know --- every
word on those pages is a word you have already met. For every answer, be ready to show me where
you see it in the table.''} Groups of 3--4, 22 minutes.

\begin{multicols}{2}
\textbf{What students do.} \emph{Scenario 1} builds the whole toolkit on one data set: count the
rows and the recorded columns (\textbf{a}), compute a mean and \emph{say what it describes}
(\textbf{b--c}), hit the first wall with ``mean position'' (\textbf{d}), then sort all five
columns into an \textbf{amount} pile and a \textbf{label} pile in their own words (\textbf{e}),
and summarize a label column with a percent instead (\textbf{f}).

\emph{Scenario 2} is the contrast case. They verify the blog's arithmetic (\textbf{a}) --- it is
correct --- and then \textbf{2b is the crux question}: the arithmetic is right, so is the
conclusion? It forces them back to their own part-\textbf{e} pile for Jersey \#. Items
\textbf{c--d} split four questions into one-look-up versus many-answers and have them rewrite one.

\columnbreak
\textbf{What the teacher does.} Circulate. Listen before speaking. When a group stalls, give a
\emph{question, cue, or prompt}, not an answer:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.1em, nosep]
  \item \textbf{1d:} ``Write Guard $+$ Forward on your paper. What is it?''
  \item \textbf{1e, Jersey \#:} ``Say it in a sentence: \emph{this player's jersey number
        is 12.} Is 12 an amount of something?''
  \item \textbf{2b (crux), if they defend the blog:} ``Compute it: 14. Now find me the player
        who wears 14 on average.''
  \item \textbf{2b, if they are stuck at `it's wrong':} ``Wrong how? Point at the row in
        part \textbf{e} that decides it.''
  \item \textbf{2c:} ``Ask two different players. Do you get two different answers?''
\end{itemize}
Pick the group work to display: one good \textbf{1c} sentence, one \textbf{1e} table with a
defended Jersey \# row, and two different \textbf{2b} answers.
\end{multicols}
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Debrief --- Formalize (what goes on the board, in a second color)]{frost}
Work through the displayed student work in order and \textbf{attach each formal name to
something a group already wrote}. Students record each line in QuickNotes as you go.
\begin{multicols}{2}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.3em, nosep, itemsep=3pt]
  \item Over the \textbf{1c} sentence: ``12 points, for the six players on the roster'' $\to$
        this is \emph{data} --- a number plus \emph{who}, \emph{what}, and \emph{units}.
  \item Over the roster: ``each row is a player,'' ``each column is a thing recorded'' $\to$
        \emph{individual} and \emph{variable}. Count aloud: 6 and 5.
  \item Over the \textbf{1e} amount pile $\to$ \emph{quantitative}; summarized by a
        \emph{mean}. Over the label pile $\to$ \emph{categorical}; summarized by
        \emph{counts and percents} (point at their \textbf{1f}, $50\%$).
  \item Over the \textbf{2b} answers $\to$ the test: \emph{does arithmetic on this variable
        mean anything?} Add grade level and ZIP code as the other classic cases.
  \item Over \textbf{2c--d} $\to$ \emph{statistical question}: its answers are expected to
        \emph{vary}, so it takes data.
\end{enumerate}
\columnbreak
\textbf{QuickNotes.} Six bullets, filled in this same order --- it is a record of what the room
just found, not a new lecture. Say each blank out loud once and move; the arguing has already
happened.

\textbf{Why this order.} Context first, because it is the only claim the warm-up already made.
Individuals and variables next, because they are just names for rows and columns the groups
counted. The two types third, because \emph{that contrast is the definition} --- do not define
them and then sort; sort and then define. The digit test fourth, because it only lands after
they have watched a correct computation produce a worthless number.

\textbf{Pose cold (the activity does not carry it):} \emph{``Siblings: 2, 0, 3, 1, 2, 4. Digits.
Categorical or quantitative?''} Quantitative --- a mean of 2 siblings is a real amount. Digits
are not the enemy; meaningless arithmetic is.
\end{multicols}
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Application --- \emph{The Student Life Survey} (worked together, 10 min)]{frost}
A fresh six-row table with a second instance of the trap (Grade: 10, 11, 9, 12, 10, 11). This is
the first place today's vocabulary is \emph{used} rather than argued into existence, and the
lesson's only in-class practice. Students hold the pen; you hold the questions:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em, nosep, itemsep=2pt]
  \item \textbf{``How many individuals, how many variables?''} --- 6 and 5. ``Student'' names
        the individuals; it is not a variable being studied. Expect ``6'' here.
  \item \textbf{``Grade is 10, 11, 9, 12 --- which type, and how do you know?''} Categorical.
        Make somebody compute the mean grade level aloud: $63/6 = 10.5$. \emph{Who is in grade
        10.5?} This is the second exposure to the crux and it must be theirs, not yours.
  \item \textbf{``What group and what units?''} --- for the Interpret line. Reject the bare
        ``18.'' The answer is \emph{these six students}, not all of Riverbend; that restriction
        is what Lesson 1.2 turns into population versus sample.
\end{itemize}
The mean commute is computed in the packet's \texttt{work} block ($108/6 = 18$ minutes).

\textbf{This is the formative check.} There is no exit ticket. Read the \emph{Interpret} and
\emph{What if} responses over shoulders and sort into three piles: \textbf{(a)} correct
\emph{and} justified from the variable's type; \textbf{(b)} correct but justified by ``it's a
number'' / ``it's words''; \textbf{(c)} missed the classification. Pile (b) is the biggest and
the most fixable. Open Lesson 1.1 by reading two pile-(b) \emph{What if} responses aloud,
unattributed.
\end{skillbox}

\boxguard
\begin{skillbox}[Watch For (while circulating)]{redbox}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em, nosep, itemsep=2pt]
  \item \textbf{The digit reflex} --- \textbf{1e}, Jersey \# row. The single most common error
        today. Ask: ``what would the average of these be, and who would that describe?''
  \item \textbf{Context-free answers} --- \textbf{1c} and the Application's \emph{Interpret}.
        A student who writes ``12'' and stops has not finished. Require the group and the units.
  \item \textbf{``It's wrong because the blog is biased''} --- \textbf{2b}. A real objection,
        but not today's. Redirect to the column: which pile is Jersey \# in, and why does that
        settle it?
  \item \textbf{``It takes data because it has a number in it''} --- \textbf{2c}. Students mark
        (i) and (iii) as \textbf{M} because they mention numbers. Redirect to variability:
        \emph{do different players give different answers?}
  \item \textbf{Height read as a label} --- \textbf{1e}. Over-correction after the Jersey \#
        argument. Ask whether 70 inches is an amount of anything.
\end{itemize}
Cold-call prompts: ``Name one individual in this table.'' ``Name one variable.'' ``Give me a
column of digits that is \emph{not} quantitative --- and one that is.'' ``Change this question
so it takes data to answer.''
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Homework --- Check Your Understanding (assigned, scored)]{goldbox}
\textbf{Northgate Community Recreation Center} --- a new context on the same spine, seven items,
about 20 minutes. Items 1--2 name individuals and variables and classify four columns; the
\textbf{contrast pair} is \emph{Member ID} (digits, label) against \emph{Age} (digits, amount),
which is the target misconception with the training wheels off. Item 3 computes a mean and
\emph{interprets} it; item 4 justifies why a percent, not a mean, summarizes ``Group class?'';
item 5 is the \textbf{spiral} (Algebra 1 percent and proportion, scaled to 2000 members); item 6
sorts four questions into statistical and not, then rewrites one; item 7 is the
\textbf{justification item} --- the mean Member ID of $140.5$ --- and cannot be answered by
computing anything. Optional extension: bring in one real claim about a group of people.

\textbf{Scored} out of 20, due at the start of the next class; the cover's score column carries
a blank for it. Score items 1--5 for correctness and items 6--7 for the \emph{reason}, not the
verdict.

\textbf{Packet or DeltaMath --- decide this lesson.} The packet set is authored either way.
DeltaMath's coverage of individuals/variables and categorical-vs-quantitative classification is
thin, and it has nothing for item 7's ``this mean measures nothing'' reasoning, so \textbf{assign
the packet pages for 1.0}. Say it aloud at Close \& Assign so it is unambiguous.
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Close \& Preview]{goldbox}
\textbf{Assign the practice} (name it: the packet's Check Your Understanding pages), then name
what changed today rather than closing on the assignment alone: \emph{``You walked in able to
compute a mean. You walk out able to say when a mean is allowed to exist --- and that is decided
by the variable, not by the arithmetic.''}

\textbf{Connections:} \textit{Covered today:} PS.DC.1a, PS.DC.1b, PS.DC.1c; AFDA.DA.2a.
\textit{Unit 1 core idea:} a conclusion is only as trustworthy as the study that produced it.
\textit{Spirals forward to:} population vs.\ sample and parameter vs.\ statistic (1.2,
PS.DC.1d--e), sampling technique (1.3, PS.DC.2), bias (1.4), and observational studies vs.\
experiments (1.5--1.7, PS.DC.3). \textit{Reaches back to:} Algebra 1 percent and proportional
reasoning.

\textbf{Preview (Lesson 1.1).} We name the four stages of the data cycle formally, write
questions a data set can actually answer, and sort variables by type at speed. Today's words ---
individual, variable, categorical, quantitative --- are assumed from here on.
\end{skillbox}

\begin{teachernote}[Warm-Up]
    Five minutes, silent, timed. All three items are Algebra 1 review and should be quick; the
    point is diagnostic, not instructional. Item 2 (scaling $18/50$ up to 900 students) is the
    one that separates the class --- students who multiply $18 \times 18$ or add rather than
    scale the \emph{proportion} need a five-minute pull-aside while the groups work. Item 3
    asks for the mean \emph{and} a sentence naming what it describes; do not accept the bare
    number, because the whole lesson turns on that sentence.

    \textbf{If it runs long, cut item 1} --- item 1f of the activity re-teaches the same percent
    in context ten minutes later.
\end{teachernote}

\begin{teachernote}[Experience \& Formalize]
    \textbf{Activity (22 min).} Split it 13/9: scenario 1 by the 13-minute mark, scenario 2 in
    the last 9. Scenario 1 is the toolkit and every answer is readable off the table, so groups
    that stall there are stalling on reading, not on mathematics --- point at the row. Announce
    the switch aloud at 13 minutes even if some groups are still on \textbf{1f}; \textbf{2b} is
    the crux and must be attempted by every group. Early finishers: send them to \textbf{2d} to
    write a second rewrite, or have them defend the ``Height'' row of \textbf{1e} to a group
    that put it in the wrong pile.

    \textbf{Debrief (14 min).} The must-land moment is move~4 --- the digit test --- and it
    lands only because they watched a correct computation (jersey mean $=14$) produce a
    worthless number. Do not shorten move~3 to protect it: sorting-then-defining is the whole
    method, and if you define categorical and quantitative first, the lesson collapses into
    the direct instruction it replaced. If time is short, cut the cold-posed ``Siblings''
    example and hand it to the Application instead.

    \textbf{Application (10 min).} The \texttt{work} block for the mean commute is already in
    the packet ($108/6 = 18$ minutes), so no board computation is needed --- spend the time on
    the three questions instead. The \emph{Grade} column is the second run at the crux; make a
    student, not you, compute $63/6 = 10.5$ aloud. Sort the \emph{Interpret} and \emph{What if}
    responses into the three piles as you circulate (correct-and-justified / correct-but-``it's
    a number'' / missed); expect roughly a third of the class in the middle pile on day one.
    That is the normal starting point, not a failure of the lesson.
\end{teachernote}

\begin{teachernote}[Homework]
    Assign all seven items. Item 3 asks for a mean \emph{and} a sentence interpreting it, and
    item 7 cannot be answered by computing anything --- flag both when assigning, because
    students who treat this as a calculation sheet will skip exactly those. Item 5 is the
    Algebra 1 spiral and is the one to check first if the warm-up showed scaling trouble.

    Grade item 7 first and only item 7 if time is short: it is the single item that separates
    students who learned the rule from students who learned the reason. Any answer that
    computes something \emph{about} $140.5$ is a miss, however well written. Go over item 2's
    Member ID row and item 7 at the start of Lesson 1.1, in that order.

    DeltaMath does not carry this lesson's skills adequately (see the Homework box), so the
    packet pages are the assignment for 1.0. The optional extension is worth collecting even
    from students who skip it --- those artifacts are reused in Lesson 1.4 for bias.
\end{teachernote}
