\boxguard[24]
\begin{scenariobox}[Millbrook Public Library --- Should Weekend Hours Be Longer?]{cerulean}
\small
Millbrook Public Library has $1{,}200$ card holders. The director wants to know
what share of them want longer weekend hours before she asks the town for the
money.

\vspace{2pt}
Her first attempt: a stack of paper surveys left on the front desk for one week,
with a box beside it. $90$ surveys come back, and $72$ of those say they want
longer weekend hours.
\end{scenariobox}

\vspace{0.08in}

\boxguard
\begin{notesbox}{Practice}
\small
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.6em, itemsep=9pt, topsep=3pt]

  \item The front-desk survey could only be filled out by someone standing at the
        front desk that week. Name two kinds of card holders who had almost no
        chance of being included.
        \writeline

        A frame that leaves out part of the population like this causes
        \blank{4cm}.

  \item Of the $90$ surveys returned, $72$ said they want longer weekend hours.

        \textbf{(a)} What percent of the returned surveys is that?

        \begin{work}
          \dfrac{72}{90} &= 0.80 \\
                         &= 80\%
        \end{work}

        \textbf{(b)} If the director applies that percent to all $1{,}200$ card
        holders, how many does she predict want longer weekend hours?

        \begin{work}
          0.80 \times 1200 &= 960 \text{ card holders}
        \end{work}

  \item A statistician talks her into doing it properly: a random sample of $120$
        card holders drawn from the full membership list, reached by phone until
        every one of them answers. $54$ say they want longer weekend hours.

        \textbf{(a)} What percent of the $120$ is that?

        \begin{work}
          \dfrac{54}{120} &= 0.45 \\
                          &= 45\%
        \end{work}

        \textbf{(b)} What does that percent predict for all $1{,}200$ card
        holders?

        \begin{work}
          0.45 \times 1200 &= 540 \text{ card holders}
        \end{work}

        \textbf{(c)} The front-desk study was too high by \blank{2cm}
        percentage points --- about \blank{2.2cm} card holders.

  \item Before the phone survey, the director had said: ``The front-desk number
        is fine, we just need more of them --- let's leave the surveys out for
        four weeks and get $360$ back.''

        Roughly what percent would those $360$ have reported, and why?
        \writelines{2}

  \item Name the kind of response bias in each situation.

        \begin{center}
        \renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.45}
        \begin{tabularx}{0.97\linewidth}{@{} X p{3.3cm} @{}}
        \toprule
        \textbf{The situation} & \textbf{Kind of response bias} \\
        \midrule
        A card holder circles ``strongly agree'' on all ten statements, two of
        which contradict each other. & \blank{2.9cm} \\
        The survey is headed \emph{``Help us win longer weekend hours!''}
          & \blank{2.9cm} \\
        The librarian who runs story hour hands out the survey and waits for it.
          & \blank{2.9cm} \\
        A card holder circles the middle box, ``no opinion,'' on every question.
          & \blank{2.9cm} \\
        \bottomrule
        \end{tabularx}
        \end{center}

  \item The director tests two orders of the same pair of questions on two
        randomly chosen halves of her sample.

        \begin{center}
        \renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.4}
        \begin{tabularx}{0.95\linewidth}{@{} p{1.7cm} X c @{}}
        \toprule
        \textbf{Version} & \textbf{Question asked first}
          & \textbf{Supported longer hours} \\
        \midrule
        A & ``Have you ever arrived to find the library closed?'' & $68\%$ \\
        B & ``Should the town raise library funding?'' & $47\%$ \\
        \bottomrule
        \end{tabularx}
        \end{center}

        The two versions differ by \blank{2cm} percentage points. Name the bias
        this shows. \blank{4cm}

  \item Name \emph{two} changes the director should make to her original
        front-desk plan, and say what each one fixes.
        \writelines{2}

  \item Suppose the phone sample of $120$ had been drawn exactly the same way,
        but only $78$ of the $120$ card holders ever picked up.

        \textbf{(a)} What percent of the sample answered? \blank{2.2cm}

        \textbf{(b)} The draw was perfectly random and every count is correct.
        Explain why the result could still be biased.
        \writelines{2}

\end{enumerate}
\end{notesbox}

\vspace{0.08in}

\boxguard
\begin{extensionbox}
\small
Find a real survey question --- a news poll, a social media poll, a customer
review form, a question from a club at school. Copy it down exactly, then:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.6em, itemsep=3pt, topsep=3pt]
  \item Say who could answer it and who is left out.
  \item Name any response bias the wording invites, and quote the words that do
        it.
  \item Rewrite the question so it does not push either way.
  \item Say which direction you think the original wording moved the results.
\end{enumerate}
\end{extensionbox}
