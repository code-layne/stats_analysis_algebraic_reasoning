    left=2mm, right=2mm, top=1.5mm, bottom=1.5mm]
    \textbf{Primary Objective:} Students will explain what makes a study
    \emph{biased} rather than merely variable, identify sampling bias
    (undercoverage and nonresponse) and the named forms of response bias in a
    given plan, show by computation that a larger sample does not reduce bias,
    and describe and justify specific changes that would reduce the bias in a
    plan.
\end{tcolorbox}

\begin{minipage}[t]{0.49\linewidth}\vspace{0pt}
    \textbf{Skills for this lesson}
    \begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.1em, itemsep=2pt, topsep=2pt]
      \item Distinguish \textbf{bias} from \textbf{sampling variability} by
            comparing repeated samples to the true value.
            \hfill \textit{PS.DC.2c}
      \item Identify \textbf{sampling bias} in a plan, naming
            \textbf{undercoverage} and \textbf{nonresponse}.
            \hfill \textit{PS.DC.2c; AFDA.DA.2e--f}
      \item Show by computation that \textbf{a larger sample does not fix
            bias}. \hfill \textit{PS.DC.2c}
      \item Name the forms of \textbf{response bias}: demand, social
            desirability, dissent, acquiescence, extreme responses, neutral
            responding, question order. \hfill \textit{PS.DC.2c}
      \item Identify the \textbf{direction} a biased plan pushes an estimate.
            \hfill \textit{PS.DC.2c; AFDA.DA.2e}
      \item Given a study plan, \textbf{name sources of bias and describe ways
            to reduce them}. \hfill \textit{AFDA.DA.2g}
    \end{itemize}
\end{minipage}
\hfill
\begin{minipage}[t]{0.47\linewidth}\vspace{0pt}
    \textbf{Key Understandings}
    \begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.1em, itemsep=2pt, topsep=2pt]
      \item \emph{Bias is a direction, not bad luck.} Lesson 1.2's honest
            samples scattered \emph{around} the parameter; a biased procedure
            misses the same way every time.
      \item A study is biased if the procedure systematically overestimates or
            underestimates the population value --- so bias is a property of the
            \textbf{method}, not of any one sample.
      \item \textbf{A large sample size does not make up for bias.} Size shrinks
            variability; nothing about size touches direction.
      \item Lesson 1.3 chose \emph{who} to ask. Response bias happens after the
            right person has already been chosen --- it is about who asks, how
            it is worded, and what order it comes in.
      \item Every fix has a price. Naming a source of bias is half the standard;
            \textbf{describing a way to reduce it} is the other half.
    \end{itemize}
\end{minipage}
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[{Vocabulary, Concepts, \& Theorems}]{greenbox}
\small
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.35}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{} >{\bfseries}p{3.1cm} X @{}}
Bias & A study is biased when its procedure systematically overestimates or
       underestimates the population value. Every sample it takes leans the
       same direction. \\
Sampling bias & Bias from how the sample was chosen: some members are
       systematically more likely to be selected than others. \\
Undercoverage & Part of the population is left off the sampling frame, so those
       members have no chance at all of being selected. \\
Nonresponse & Individuals are chosen properly but never answer, so the returned
       data is missing exactly the people who stayed silent. \\
Response bias & Anything about the survey itself --- who asks, the wording, the
       order --- that pushes a respondent away from their true answer. \\
Forms of response bias & Demand, social desirability, dissent, acquiescence,
       extreme responses, neutral responding, question order. \\
Question order bias & An earlier question changes the answer to a later one, so
       reordering the same questions changes the result. \\
Precision vs.\ accuracy & A larger sample buys precision (less scatter). Only a
       better procedure buys accuracy (no lean). \\
\end{tabularx}
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Hook]{frost}
    Put the replanned pool study on the board --- the good one the class built
    last period: numbered frame, chance choosing, membership types in
    proportion. Then add one detail: \textbf{the surveys are handed out at the
    front desk with the pool director standing beside the table, watching.}
    \textbf{Ask: the sample is perfect. Name one reason the answers could still
    be wrong.} Wait through the silence --- students have spent a full lesson
    being told that a good sampling method is the fix, and this is the first
    time that promise fails. Somebody will say ``people won't complain in front
    of her.'' Write it down and give it its name later. The point to land before
    the notes open: \textbf{Lesson 1.3 fixed \emph{who gets asked}. It cannot
    fix \emph{what they say}.} Everything today lives in that gap.
\end{skillbox}

\boxguard
\begin{skillbox}[Lesson]{frost}
\begin{multicols}{2}
\textbf{1. Bias is a direction.} Start with the two-row table, because it does
the whole conceptual job visually. Both rows wobble; only one is wrong the same
way every time. \textbf{Ask before revealing the averages: which row would you
trust, and why?} Students reliably say ``the one that agrees with itself'' ---
the Tuesday row, whose numbers are tightest. That wrong answer is worth two
minutes: consistency is not accuracy. Then compute the averages and put $20\%$
beside them.

\textbf{2. Sampling bias.} Undercoverage first, since it is 1.3's frame idea
wearing a new name. Run the four-row table fast. Save the argument for row 4:
the frame is \emph{perfect} --- all $1{,}500$ got a card --- and the study is
still broken. \textbf{Ask what is different about the $200$ who mailed theirs
back.} That is nonresponse, and it is the row students will meet again on the
activity and twice on the homework.

\textbf{3. Size does not fix bias.} The arithmetic is one division and takes a
minute; the idea takes the rest. Do the division, fill in $16$ points twice, and
then say the sentence out loud and have them write it: \emph{a larger sample
makes an estimate more precise, not more correct.} This is the single most
transferable line in Unit 1 and it returns in Unit 7 as the difference between a
narrow interval and a right one.

\textbf{4. Response bias.} Seven names is a lot; do not lecture them. Read the
reference table once, then go straight to the four diagnostic rows and let the
class argue. Rows 2 and 3 (neutral responding vs.\ extreme responses) are the
pair worth slowing down on --- they are opposites and students conflate them
with ``didn't care.''

\textbf{5. Question order, and fixes.} The $62\%$/$41\%$ pair sells itself:
identical words, $21$ points apart. Then the four fixes --- this is AFDA.DA.2g
and it is the part that gets assessed on the homework. Insist each fix be
attached to the bias it removes; ``ask better questions'' is not an answer.

\textbf{Guided practice.} Three plans, a bias, and a fix each. The gym row is
undercoverage, the principal row is social desirability, the mail row is
nonresponse. Assign the closing write for homework if time runs out --- but do
not skip it, it is the exit ticket in miniature.
\end{multicols}
\end{skillbox}

\boxguard
\begin{skillbox}[Explicit Instruction: Diagnosing Bias in a Study Plan]{frost}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{} p{0.46\linewidth} X @{}}
\begin{minipage}[t]{\linewidth}\vspace{0pt}
\textbf{Steps --- give these verbatim}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.4em, itemsep=2pt, topsep=2pt]
  \item \textbf{Who is on the frame?} If part of the population cannot be
        reached at all, that is \textbf{undercoverage}.
  \item \textbf{Who was chosen but never answered?} That is
        \textbf{nonresponse}.
  \item \textbf{Who is asking, and is anyone watching?} That points to
        \textbf{social desirability}.
  \item \textbf{What do the words tell the respondent to say?} That points to
        \textbf{demand bias} or \textbf{acquiescence}.
  \item \textbf{What came before this question?} That points to
        \textbf{question order bias}.
  \item \textbf{Which way does it push?} Say \emph{too high} or \emph{too low},
        and name who is over- or under-counted.
  \item \textbf{Name the fix and what it removes.} A bias named with no fix is
        half an answer; a fix named with no bias is not an answer.
\end{enumerate}
\end{minipage}
&
\begin{minipage}[t]{\linewidth}\vspace{0pt}
\textbf{Worked example --- ``A library leaves surveys on the front desk for a
week. $90$ come back; $72$ want longer weekend hours, so the director reports
$80\%$ of $1{,}200$ card holders --- about $960$ people.''}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.1em, itemsep=2pt, topsep=2pt]
  \item[1.] The frame is \emph{people at the desk that week}, not card holders
        --- undercoverage of everyone who did not visit.
  \item[2.] Of those who saw it, only the motivated filled it out ---
        nonresponse on top of undercoverage.
  \item[3--5.] Nobody watching, neutral wording, one question --- no response
        bias here. The damage is all in the frame.
  \item[6.] \textbf{Too high.} The people who want longer hours are exactly the
        ones who visit often and bother to write.
  \item[7.] \textbf{Fix:} draw a random sample from the full card-holder list
        (removes undercoverage) and call back the ones who do not reply
        (removes nonresponse).
\end{itemize}
\textit{The proper phone survey came back $45\%$ --- $540$ people, not $960$.
The first study was not a little off; it was off by $420$ card holders.}
\end{minipage}
\end{tabularx}
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Active Monitoring]{redbox}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em, itemsep=2pt, topsep=2pt]
  \item \textbf{Watch for ``biased'' used to mean ``prejudiced.''} Students
        arrive with the everyday meaning and write ``the survey was unfair to
        seniors.'' Redirect every time to the statistical meaning: \emph{which
        direction does the number miss, and by how much?}
  \item \textbf{Watch for ``the sample was too small.''} This is the reflex
        answer to every flawed study in the lesson and it is wrong in all of
        them. Diagnostic: \emph{if they asked ten times as many the same way,
        what would change?} Section 3 exists for this student.
  \item \textbf{Watch for undercoverage and nonresponse fused.} Both leave
        people out of the data. The split is whether they were ever \emph{on the
        frame}. Ask: \emph{could that person have been chosen?} Yes but silent
        $\Rightarrow$ nonresponse. No $\Rightarrow$ undercoverage.
  \item \textbf{Watch for a bias named without a direction.} ``Nonresponse''
        alone earns half credit on the activity and homework; the standard is
        satisfied when the student says which way the estimate moves and who is
        over-counted.
  \item \textbf{Watch for neutral responding read as extreme.} The two are
        opposites. Point at the actual boxes the respondent circled.
  \item \textbf{Cold-call prompts:} ``Could that person have been chosen at
        all?'' ``Who is missing from the data?'' ``Which direction does it
        push?'' ``What would ten times as many change?'' ``What does your fix
        remove?''
\end{itemize}
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Group Work \& Differentiation]{redbox}
\small
\begin{multicols}{3}
\textbf{Tier R --- Remediate}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1em, itemsep=1pt, topsep=2pt]
  \item Name the population and how many households answered.
  \item Match five plans to their source of bias, including one with a perfect
        frame.
  \item Compute the return rate $150 \div 600 = 25\%$ and the complaint rate
        $96 \div 150 = 64\%$.
\end{itemize}

\columnbreak
\textbf{Tier A --- Approaching Proficiency}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1em, itemsep=1pt, topsep=2pt]
  \item Scale $64\%$ to $384$ households, then compare to the census truth of
        $40\%$ ($240$).
  \item Re-run with twice the cards ($192/300$) and explain the unchanged
        $64\%$.
  \item Name the bias a leading question invites, then rewrite it neutrally.
\end{itemize}

\columnbreak
\textbf{Tier E --- Extension}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1em, itemsep=1pt, topsep=2pt]
  \item Explain why the $450$ silent households bias the result, and which way.
  \item Reason from unit type to the \emph{direction} of the error.
  \item Name what each of three competing fixes removes --- and what it costs.
\end{itemize}
\end{multicols}
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Individual Work \& Assessment]{redbox}
\textbf{Exit ticket (3 items, no notes).} One context: Bayside Middle School,
$750$ students, the lunch period.
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.4em, itemsep=2pt, topsep=2pt]
  \item Name the source of bias in three plans --- one undercoverage, one
        nonresponse, one social desirability.
  \item Compute $24 \div 30 = 80\%$, then $48 \div 60 = 80\%$, and say whether
        doubling the sample fixed anything (it did not --- same library, same
        missing students).
  \item \textbf{Conceptual / justification check:} name what is wrong with
        \emph{``Don't you agree that our rushed $25$-minute lunch should be
        longer?''} and rewrite it neutrally. Correct answer names the leading
        phrasing (``Don't you agree,'' ``rushed'') and the acquiescence it
        invites, then offers a balanced replacement. Grade this one first.
\end{enumerate}
\textbf{Using the results:} sort into three piles --- (a) named the wording
problem \emph{and} produced a genuinely neutral rewrite, (b) named the problem
but rewrote it leading the other way (``Is our lunch period long enough?''
with no ``no'' offered), (c) rewrote it without naming anything. Pile (b) is the
interesting one and is worth two minutes at the start of Lesson 1.5: they have
seen that wording matters but not yet that \emph{balance} is what fixes it.
\end{skillbox}

\boxguard
\begin{skillbox}[Reinforcement \& Extension]{goldbox}
\textbf{Homework --- Millbrook Public Library, two studies and two answers.} The
same question is studied twice: a front-desk pile that returns $80\%$ and a
proper random phone sample that returns $45\%$. Students name the undercoverage,
compute both percents and both scaled predictions ($960$ and $540$), quantify
the gap ($35$ points, $420$ card holders), predict what four weeks of the same
flawed collection would have produced, match four response biases, compute a
$21$-point question-order gap, propose two fixes with what each removes, and
close by explaining why a perfectly random draw with a $65\%$ response rate can
still be biased.

\textbf{Extension (optional).} Students find a real survey question --- a news
poll, a social media poll, a review form --- and critique it: who could answer,
what bias the wording invites (quoting the words), a neutral rewrite, and the
direction they think the original moved the result. Collect these; with the 1.2
and 1.3 extensions they are most of a proposal for the Lesson 1.8 class-survey
project.

\textbf{Preview of Lesson 1.5.} Every study in Unit 1 so far has only
\emph{watched}. Next class names that: observational studies --- what they can
support, what they cannot, and why ``these two things go together'' is not
``one caused the other'' (PS.DC.2d).

\textbf{Connections \& Big Ideas.} \textit{Unit 1 core idea:} a conclusion is
only as trustworthy as the study that produced it. \textit{Standards covered:}
PS.DC.2c; AFDA.DA.2e, AFDA.DA.2f, AFDA.DA.2g. \textit{Spirals forward to:}
observational studies (1.5, PS.DC.2d), control and randomization in experimental
design (1.6, PS.DC.3a--b), choosing a collection method (1.7), the full-cycle
project (1.8), and --- directly --- the conditions on inference in Units 7 and 8,
where a biased sample invalidates an interval no matter how narrow it is.
\textit{Reaches back to:} Lesson 1.2's sampling variability (the contrast that
opens this lesson) and Lesson 1.3's sampling frame.
\end{skillbox}

\begin{teachernote}[Warm-Up]
    Five minutes, silent, timed. Items 1 and 2 are Algebra 1 percent work and
    should be near-universal. Item 3 is the one that matters.

    Item 3(a) is a straight ``percent of'' computation, $0.36 \times 1500 = 540$.
    Item 3(b) asks students to compare $540$ against the $300$ they computed in
    item 1 --- and this is the first time in Unit 1 that a sample statistic is
    put beside a \emph{known} parameter. Do not explain why yet. Collect the
    warm-up with the answers ``$240$, too high'' on the board and leave them
    there; section 1 of the notes points back at them.

    Watch for students who answer 3(b) with a percent ($16$ points) instead of a
    count. Both are right ideas but the item asks for members; the percentage
    form is exactly what section 3 will want, so credit it and tell them to hold
    the thought.
\end{teachernote}

\begin{teachernote}[Guided Notes]
    Pace: section 1 in 10 minutes, section 2 in 9, section 3 in 8, section 4 in
    12, section 5 in 8, guided practice in 8. \textbf{Sections 1 and 3 are the
    lesson}; section 4 is the longest but the least difficult.

    Section 1's table is the conceptual heart and it fails if you reveal the
    averages too early. Ask which row they would trust \emph{first}. The tight
    Tuesday row draws the vote, and the correction --- consistent is not the
    same as correct --- is the idea the whole lesson rests on. Budget two
    minutes for it.

    Section 2 row 4 is the one that argues: every member got a card, so the
    frame is perfect, and the study is still wrong. If students insist that is
    the same as undercoverage, use the test question --- \emph{could that person
    have been chosen?} --- and hold the line. The activity's entire premise is
    this row.

    Section 4's seven names are recognition-level, not recall-level. Do not
    lecture the reference table; read it once and go to the four diagnostic
    rows. The pair to slow down on is neutral responding versus extreme
    responses, which are opposites and get fused into ``didn't take it
    seriously.''

    If you are short on time, compress section 4 (the homework re-teaches it
    with four more rows) --- never section 1 or section 3.
\end{teachernote}

\begin{teachernote}[Group Activity]
    Groups of three, 15--18 minutes, tiers assigned rather than chosen. The
    headline box is doing real work: \emph{every} household got a card. Read
    that sentence aloud when you assign, because Tier R item 5 and all of Tier E
    depend on students believing the frame was perfect.

    Tier R item 2 is fast; circulate instead on items 3 and 4, where the two
    fractions have different denominators on purpose. $150/600$ is the return
    rate and $96/150$ is the complaint rate; groups that divide $96$ by $600$
    have merged the two questions and will get $16\%$. That error is worth
    catching at the table.

    Tier A item 1(c) is the payoff for the whole tier: $384$ predicted against
    $240$ actual, a gap of $24$ points and $144$ households. Tier A item 2
    then re-runs the study at double size and lands on $64\%$ again. Groups that
    reach 2(b) and write ``more of a biased sample is still biased'' have the
    lesson.

    Tier E item 2 is the best item on the page and the hardest: two-bedroom
    households own more cars \emph{and} answered at twice the rate, so the
    complaint rate among returned cards beats the real one. Students must chain
    two facts to get the direction. Pull it to the whole class if two or more
    groups reach it. Tier E item 3 is where the ``just do it properly'' answer
    meets reality: insist on \emph{both} halves of each line, because the cost
    is the half students skip --- $450$ door knocks is not free.
\end{teachernote}

\begin{teachernote}[Exit Ticket]
    Five minutes, notes closed, collected at the door. Item 1 should be near
    universal; if the nonresponse row is widely missed, section 2 row 4 did not
    land and needs three minutes at the start of Lesson 1.5.

    Item 2(b) is the one that separates the class. The percent is trivial ---
    $48/60 = 80\%$ --- and the explanation is the assessment. ``No, because it
    is still only library students'' is full credit. ``No, because $60$ is still
    too small'' is the section-3 misconception surviving intact; write the
    correction on the paper rather than marking it, because these students will
    carry the same error into Unit 7.

    Grade item 3 first and only item 3 if time is short. Full credit needs both
    halves: naming the leading phrasing \emph{and} a rewrite that is genuinely
    balanced. Watch for the rewrite that simply flips the lean (``Is our lunch
    period long enough?'' with no ``no'' option offered) --- it shows the student
    has learned that wording matters without yet seeing that balance is the fix.
    That is the productive pile and it is worth two minutes next class.
\end{teachernote}

\begin{teachernote}[Homework]
    Assign all eight items; about $30$ minutes. Items 4, 7, and 8 cannot be
    answered by computing anything --- flag all three when assigning, because
    students who treat this as a calculation sheet will skip exactly those.

    Items 2 and 3 are the spine and are designed to be done in order: $80\%$ and
    $960$, then $45\%$ and $540$, then the gap in 3(c) --- $35$ points and $420$
    card holders. A student who computes 3(c) as $960 - 540$ and as $80 - 45$
    has understood that percentage points and counts are two views of the same
    error. Both are asked for; accept either order.

    Item 4 is the transfer item for section 3 and the answer is a number
    (``about $80\%$ again'') plus a reason. Students who predict something
    closer to $45\%$ believe more data drifts toward truth on its own; that is
    the misconception to correct in writing.

    Item 8 closes the arc the hook opened. The draw was perfectly random and the
    counts are all correct, so the answer cannot be ``they sampled badly.'' It
    has to be that a random draw decides who is \emph{asked}, not who
    \emph{answers} --- and the $42$ who never picked up may use the library
    differently from the $78$ who did. Students who write ``$78$ is too small''
    have the wrong lever again; ask them what would change if the library called
    $1{,}200$ people and $780$ answered.

    The \textbf{remindbox} hands off to Lesson 1.5: every study in this unit so
    far has only watched, never changed anything. Do not open causation here.
\end{teachernote}
