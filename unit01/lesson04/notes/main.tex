\begin{notesbox}{1. Bias Is a Direction, Not Bad Luck}
\small
Lesson 1.2 said that honest random samples disagree with each other --- that is
\textbf{sampling variability}, and nobody is wrong. Those samples scatter
\emph{around} the true value.

\vspace{2pt}
A study is \textbf{biased} when its procedure \emph{systematically} overestimates
or underestimates the population value. Every sample it takes leans the
\textbf{same direction}.

\vspace{3pt}
The pool office's own records say $20\%$ of all $1{,}500$ members mainly swim
laps. Here are two ways of taking three samples of $75$:

\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.4}
\begin{tabularx}{0.98\linewidth}{@{} X c c c p{2.1cm} @{}}
\toprule
\textbf{How the $75$ were chosen} & \textbf{1st} & \textbf{2nd} & \textbf{3rd}
  & \textbf{Average} \\
\midrule
At random from the full membership list & $19\%$ & $22\%$ & $19\%$
  & \blank{1.7cm} \\
From whoever arrived Tuesday evening & $35\%$ & $36\%$ & $37\%$
  & \blank{1.7cm} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}

\vspace{3pt}
Both rows disagree with themselves --- that is variability, and it is unavoidable.
Only one row disagrees with the \emph{truth} every single time.

\vspace{2pt}
The biased row is the \blank{4cm} row, and it misses \blank{2.2cm}
(too high / too low).

\vspace{3pt}
\textit{Variability is noise: it shrinks when the sample grows. Bias is a
direction: it does not.}
\end{notesbox}

\boxguard[30]
\begin{notesbox}{2. Sampling Bias --- Who Never Had a Chance}
\small
The \textbf{sampling frame} is the list a study can actually choose from.
\textbf{Sampling bias} happens when some members of the population are
systematically more likely to be chosen than others.

\vspace{2pt}
Two ways it gets in:

\vspace{2pt}
\textbf{Undercoverage} --- part of the population is left off the frame entirely,
so those members can never be chosen. \textbf{Nonresponse} --- members are chosen
properly but do not answer, so the data that comes back is missing exactly the
people who stayed silent.

\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.45}
\begin{tabularx}{0.98\linewidth}{@{} X p{4.3cm} @{}}
\toprule
\textbf{The plan} & \textbf{Who is missing?} \\
\midrule
Ask the members who arrive Tuesday evening. & \blank{3.8cm} \\
Email the survey only to members with an address on file. & \blank{3.8cm} \\
Post the survey on the pool's social media page. & \blank{3.8cm} \\
Mail a card to all $1{,}500$ members; $200$ mail it back. & \blank{3.8cm} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}

\vspace{3pt}
The first three rows are \textbf{undercoverage} --- the frame itself is too
small. The last row has a perfect frame and still has a problem: it is
\blank{3.6cm}.

\vspace{2pt}
\textit{Nobody left off the frame can ever be sampled, and nobody who stays
silent can ever be counted. Neither one shows up in your arithmetic.}
\end{notesbox}

\boxguard[26]
\begin{notesbox}{3. A Bigger Sample Does Not Fix Bias}
\small
The staff member is told her sample was too small, so she runs the same Tuesday
evening survey every week for ten weeks. She ends up asking $750$ members, and
$270$ of them say they mainly swim laps.

\begin{work}
  \dfrac{270}{750} &= 0.36 \\
                   &= 36\%
\end{work}

\vspace{2pt}
Ten times the work, and the same answer. Fill in the comparison:

\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.4}
\begin{tabularx}{0.95\linewidth}{@{} c c c X @{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Members asked} & \textbf{Study says} & \textbf{Truth}
  & \textbf{Off by how many percentage points?} \\
\midrule
$75$  & $36\%$ & $20\%$ & \blank{4cm} \\
$750$ & $36\%$ & $20\%$ & \blank{4cm} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}

\vspace{3pt}
A larger sample makes an estimate more \blank{2.6cm}, but it cannot make a
biased procedure \blank{2.6cm}. Sample size fixes
\blank{3.4cm}; it does nothing to bias.

\vspace{2pt}
\textit{Said plainly: a bigger biased sample is a more confident wrong answer.}
\end{notesbox}

\boxguard[30]
\begin{notesbox}{4. Response Bias --- When the Answer Is Not the Truth}
\small
Everything so far went wrong before anyone spoke. \textbf{Response bias} is
anything about the survey itself --- who is asking, how it is worded, what order
it comes in --- that pushes a respondent away from their real answer.

\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.3}
\begin{tabularx}{0.98\linewidth}{@{} >{\bfseries}p{3.4cm} X @{}}
\toprule
\textbf{\normalfont Kind} & \textbf{What the respondent does} \\
\midrule
Demand bias & Guesses what the study wants to hear and answers to match. \\
Social desirability & Gives the answer that looks good to other people. \\
Dissent bias & Answers negatively to everything, often without understanding
  the questions. \\
Acquiescence bias & Answers positively to everything --- agrees with it all. \\
Extreme responses & Always picks ``strongly agree'' or ``strongly disagree.'' \\
Neutral responding & Always picks the middle, no-opinion choice. \\
Question order bias & An earlier question changes the answer to a later one. \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}

\vspace{3pt}
Name the kind of response bias in each situation:

\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.45}
\begin{tabularx}{0.98\linewidth}{@{} X p{3.6cm} @{}}
\toprule
\textbf{At the pool} & \textbf{Kind of response bias} \\
\midrule
The head lifeguard hands out the survey and waits. Members who complained last
week now rate the staff ``excellent.'' & \blank{3.1cm} \\
A member marks ``no opinion'' on all fifteen questions. & \blank{3.1cm} \\
A member marks ``strongly agree'' on every question, including two that
contradict each other. & \blank{3.1cm} \\
The survey is titled \emph{``Help us show the pool needs longer hours.''}
& \blank{3.1cm} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}

\vspace{2pt}
\textit{Response bias does not care how you drew the sample. It happens after
the right person has already been chosen.}
\end{notesbox}

\boxguard[30]
\begin{notesbox}{5. Question Order --- and How to Reduce Bias}
\small
The board sends out two versions of the same two-question survey. The questions
are word-for-word identical; only the \textbf{order} is different.

\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.4}
\begin{tabularx}{0.98\linewidth}{@{} p{1.9cm} X c @{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Version} & \textbf{Order of the two questions}
  & \textbf{Said yes to the \$40,000} \\
\midrule
A & ``Are the lap lanes overcrowded?'' \emph{then} ``Should the town spend
    \$40,000 to add a lane?'' & $62\%$ \\
B & ``Should the town spend \$40,000 to add a lane?'' \emph{then} ``Are the lap
    lanes overcrowded?'' & $41\%$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}

\vspace{2pt}
The two versions differ by \blank{2.6cm} percentage points, on the same
question, from the same membership. This is \blank{4cm}.

\vspace{3pt}
\textbf{Four ways to reduce bias in a plan.} Fill in what each one fixes:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.6em, itemsep=4pt, topsep=3pt]
  \item Build the frame from the \emph{complete} membership list, not from
        whoever shows up. \\ Fixes: \blank{5.5cm}
  \item Go back to the members who did not answer the first time. \\
        Fixes: \blank{5.5cm}
  \item Word every question neutrally --- never ``Don't you agree that\dots'' \\
        Fixes: \blank{5.5cm}
  \item Let members answer anonymously, with nobody watching. \\
        Fixes: \blank{5.5cm}
\end{enumerate}
\end{notesbox}
