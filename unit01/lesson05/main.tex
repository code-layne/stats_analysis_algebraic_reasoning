    \textbf{Primary Objective:} Students will explain what makes a study
    \emph{observational}, use the statistical cycle to plan and carry out an
    observational study, compute and compare rates for two groups that already
    exist, describe the result as an \emph{association}, and justify which
    conclusions the study does and does not support --- naming a lurking
    variable or a reversed arrow when it does not.
\end{tcolorbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Priority Ideas \& Skills]{goldbox}
\begin{minipage}[t]{0.49\linewidth}\vspace{0pt}
    \textbf{Skills for this lesson}
    \begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.1em, itemsep=2pt, topsep=2pt]
      \item Identify a study as \textbf{observational} by asking whether anyone
            \emph{assigned} the groups. \hfill \textit{PS.DC.2d}
      \item Use the \textbf{statistical cycle} to plan an observational study:
            formulate, collect, represent, analyze.
            \hfill \textit{PS.DC.2d; PS.DC.1a}
      \item Compute a rate for each of two existing groups and report the
            \textbf{gap in percentage points}. \hfill \textit{PS.DC.2d}
      \item Name the \textbf{explanatory} and \textbf{response} variables in a
            reported finding. \hfill \textit{PS.DC.2d}
      \item State a result as an \textbf{association}, using no causal verb.
            \hfill \textit{PS.DC.2d; PS.DC.1b}
      \item Explain why an association does not establish \textbf{causation},
            naming a reversed arrow or a \textbf{lurking variable}.
            \hfill \textit{PS.DC.2d}
    \end{itemize}
\end{minipage}
\hfill
\begin{minipage}[t]{0.47\linewidth}\vspace{0pt}
    \textbf{Key Understandings}
    \begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.1em, itemsep=2pt, topsep=2pt]
      \item \emph{Observational studies examine relationships and patterns in a
            population} --- and a survey is one type of them, so every study in
            Unit 1 so far has been observational.
      \item In an observational study the researcher \textbf{does not assign the
            treatment}. The groups formed themselves, which is the entire reason
            causation is out of reach.
      \item \textbf{An unbiased study is not the same as a conclusive one.}
            Lesson 1.4 removed bias; a perfectly clean sample still cannot say
            \emph{why} two groups differ.
      \item The cycle does not change from study to study. What changes is what
            you are licensed to write at the end of stage four.
      \item Every association has three live explanations: the claim, the same
            arrow \emph{backwards}, and a \textbf{lurking variable} moving both.
            Ruling any of them out requires assigning --- Lesson 1.6.
    \end{itemize}
\end{minipage}
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[{Vocabulary, Concepts, \& Theorems}]{greenbox}
\small
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.35}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{} >{\bfseries}p{3.1cm} X @{}}
Observational study & A study in which you measure or survey the members of a
       sample without trying to affect them. The researcher does not assign
       anyone to a group. \\
Survey & One type of observational study: the members of a sample are asked
       questions and their answers are recorded. \\
Explanatory variable & The variable we think might explain or account for the
       other one --- the tail of the arrow in the claim being tested. \\
Response variable & The variable we measure to see what happens --- the head of
       the arrow. \\
Association & Two variables move together: knowing which group an individual is
       in changes what you expect for the other variable. \\
Causation & One variable actually produces the change in the other. A far
       stronger claim than association. \\
Lurking variable & A variable outside the study that affects both variables in
       it, and can create an association all by itself. \\
Reversed arrow & The response variable is what put individuals into their group
       in the first place, so the association runs the other way. \\
\end{tabularx}
\end{skillbox}

\boxguard
\begin{skillbox}[Hook]{frost}
    Put the warm-up's two percents on the board and add the director's flyer:
    \emph{``Swim in the morning --- sleep better!''} Then close every door the
    class has learned to open. \textbf{The sample was random. Nobody was left
    off the list. Everyone answered. Every count is correct.} \textbf{Ask: is
    the flyer safe?} Expect a long pause, then a split room --- and that split
    is the lesson. Students spent Lesson 1.4 learning that a clean procedure is
    the fix, and this is the first time a clean procedure is not enough. Do not
    resolve it. Write both sides of the room on the board and say only: today we
    find out what a study that \emph{watches} is allowed to claim.
\end{skillbox}

\boxguard
\begin{skillbox}[Lesson]{frost}
\begin{multicols}{2}
\textbf{1. One word: assign.} Run the four-plan table fast --- it is a sorting
task, not a thinking task, and the only row that matters is row 3, where the
director assigns swim times. \textbf{Ask on every row: who decided which group
that person is in?} Land the definition from the standard's own words: you
measure or survey without trying to affect anyone. Then the line students should
write down: \emph{a survey is one type of observational study}, so every study
in Unit 1 has been one.

\textbf{2. The cycle, again.} This is PS.DC.2d proper and it should feel like
review, because it is --- the same four stages from Lesson 1.1, with 1.3's
sampling plan in the Collect row and 1.4's bias check standing behind it.
\textbf{Ask what is missing from the plan.} Nothing tells a member when to swim.
That absence is the whole definition, written as a plan.

\textbf{3. The table.} Two group rates with two different denominators, from the
warm-up. Keep it quick --- the arithmetic is done. Spend the time on
explanatory/response, which is new vocabulary and pays forward to scatterplots
in Unit 5. The $825$ computation is there to keep the ``scale a sample up to the
population'' skill alive from 1.2.

\textbf{4. Three stories.} The heart of the lesson, and it needs the full block.
Give story 1, then \textbf{ask the class for a way the arrow could run
backwards} before offering one --- somebody always finds it. Then reveal the
retirement row: $75\%$ against $15\%$. Let them sit with it. Working a job
changes both when you can swim and how long you sleep, and it was never in the
study at all.

\textbf{5. What you may claim.} Three statements, one supported. The third
(``if they switched\dots'') is the subtle one --- a prediction about a change is
a causal claim wearing different clothes. Close on the two write-up rules and
the banned verbs: \emph{causes, makes, raises, improves}.

\textbf{Guided practice.} Firefighters and fire damage is the row that gets a
laugh and does the most work; shoe size and reading is on the homework as its
twin. End on the sports-and-grades write --- it is the exit ticket in miniature.
\end{multicols}
\end{skillbox}

\boxguard
\begin{skillbox}[Explicit Instruction: Reading a Claim from an Observational Study]{frost}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{} p{0.46\linewidth} X @{}}
\begin{minipage}[t]{\linewidth}\vspace{0pt}
\textbf{Steps --- give these verbatim}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.4em, itemsep=2pt, topsep=2pt]
  \item \textbf{Did anyone assign the groups?} No $\Rightarrow$ observational.
        Yes $\Rightarrow$ experiment (Lesson 1.6).
  \item \textbf{Name the two variables.} Explanatory (the suspected cause),
        response (what was measured).
  \item \textbf{Compute a rate for each group} --- watch the denominators, they
        are different on purpose.
  \item \textbf{Report the gap} in percentage points.
  \item \textbf{Write the association sentence:} both percents, the gap, and no
        causal verb.
  \item \textbf{Test the claim three ways.} Could the arrow run backwards?
        Could a lurking variable move both? Only then, could the claim be true?
  \item \textbf{Say what would settle it:} someone would have to \emph{assign}
        the groups.
\end{enumerate}
\end{minipage}
&
\begin{minipage}[t]{\linewidth}\vspace{0pt}
\textbf{Worked example --- ``Riverbend samples $150$ records. Of the $60$ who
used the Study Center, $39$ passed every class; of the $90$ who did not, $72$
did. The principal writes: the Study Center is hurting grades.''}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.1em, itemsep=2pt, topsep=2pt]
  \item[1.] Nobody was assigned --- students walked in. Observational.
  \item[2.] Explanatory: using the center. Response: passing every class.
  \item[3.] $39/60 = 65\%$ and $72/90 = 80\%$.
  \item[4.] A gap of $15$ points, with the \emph{non}-users higher.
  \item[5.] \textit{At Riverbend, $65\%$ of Study Center users passed every
        class against $80\%$ of non-users --- using the center is associated
        with a lower pass rate.}
  \item[6.] The arrow runs backwards: struggling students are the ones who go.
        The records prove it --- $75\%$ of users had already failed a class,
        against $10\%$ of non-users.
  \item[7.] Assign students to attend at random, or compare each student to
        their own previous quarter.
\end{itemize}
\textit{The memo has the direction of the arrow exactly reversed. The center
did not lower the grades; the low grades sent the students to the center.}
\end{minipage}
\end{tabularx}
\end{skillbox}

\boxguard
\begin{skillbox}[Active Monitoring]{redbox}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em, itemsep=2pt, topsep=2pt]
  \item \textbf{Watch for ``the study was biased'' as the all-purpose answer.}
        It is Lesson 1.4's tool applied to a lesson that deliberately removed
        bias from every scenario. Redirect: \emph{the sample was random and
        everyone answered --- so what is left?}
  \item \textbf{Watch for causal verbs sneaking into a written answer.} Circle
        \emph{causes, makes, raises, improves, helps, hurts} wherever they
        appear and hand the paper back. The banned-verb list is the fastest
        formative check on the page.
  \item \textbf{Watch for a lurking variable that is really one of the two
        variables.} ``Study Center users study less'' is not a third variable.
        Test: \emph{is it in the table? If yes, it is not lurking.}
  \item \textbf{Watch for the reversed arrow being missed entirely.} Most
        students accept the direction they are handed. Prompt: \emph{which one
        came first?}
  \item \textbf{Watch for ``no'' with no reason.} Half credit. The standard is
        met when the student names a specific alternative explanation for
        \emph{this} context.
  \item \textbf{Cold-call prompts:} ``Who decided the groups?'' ``Which came
        first?'' ``What is in this study, and what is outside it?'' ``What would
        you have to do to settle it?'' ``Say that again without the word
        \emph{causes}.''
\end{itemize}
\end{skillbox}

\boxguard
\begin{skillbox}[Group Work \& Differentiation]{redbox}
\small
\begin{multicols}{3}
\textbf{Tier R --- Remediate}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1em, itemsep=1pt, topsep=2pt]
  \item Name the population, the sample, and both group sizes.
  \item Decide it is observational and say who decided the groups.
  \item Compute $39 \div 60 = 65\%$ and $72 \div 90 = 80\%$.
  \item Say which group is higher, and by how many points.
\end{itemize}

\columnbreak
\textbf{Tier A --- Approaching Proficiency}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1em, itemsep=1pt, topsep=2pt]
  \item Pool the sample: $111/150 = 74\%$, scaled to $666$ of $900$.
  \item Name the explanatory and response variables.
  \item Write the association sentence with no causal verb.
  \item Explain why the principal's memo is not supported.
\end{itemize}

\columnbreak
\textbf{Tier E --- Extension}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1em, itemsep=1pt, topsep=2pt]
  \item Compute $75\%$ against $10\%$ on the previous quarter's records.
  \item Reason to the \emph{direction}: the center looks worse than it is.
  \item Weigh two better designs --- what each fixes and what it costs.
\end{itemize}
\end{multicols}
\end{skillbox}

\boxguard
\begin{skillbox}[Individual Work \& Assessment]{redbox}
\textbf{Exit ticket (3 items, no notes).} One context: Bayside Middle School,
$750$ students, band and math grades.
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.4em, itemsep=2pt, topsep=2pt]
  \item Identify the study as observational and say how they know (nobody was
        placed in band --- students signed up).
  \item Compute $90 \div 150 = 60\%$ and $240 \div 600 = 40\%$, then the
        $20$-point gap. The two denominators are the whole difficulty.
  \item \textbf{Conceptual / justification check:} the principal concludes band
        \emph{raises} math grades and wants it required. Full credit says no
        \emph{and} names a specific alternative --- students who sign up may
        already study more, or families with time for band have time for
        homework. Grade this one first.
\end{enumerate}
\textbf{Using the results:} sort into three piles --- (a) said no and named a
concrete alternative explanation, (b) said no because ``you can't prove
causation'' with nothing attached to this context, (c) said yes. Pile (b) is the
one to watch: they have the slogan without the reasoning, and they will lose it
the moment a context looks convincing. Open Lesson 1.6 by handing pile (b) the
same table and asking what would have to change about \emph{how students got
into band} for the conclusion to hold.
\end{skillbox}

\boxguard
\begin{skillbox}[Reinforcement \& Extension]{goldbox}
\textbf{Homework --- Millbrook Public Library, does the reading program work?}
An unbiased phone study of $240$ card holders: $80\%$ of the $90$ who signed up
for the Summer Reading Program read at least $10$ books, against $32\%$ of the
$150$ who did not. Students identify the study as observational, compute both
rates and the $48$-point gap, pool the sample ($120/240 = 50\%$, scaled to $600$
of $1{,}200$), name the explanatory and response variables, write the
association sentence, give two non-causal explanations for the gap, match four
associations to lurking variables, compute the previous-summer rates ($70\%$
against $20\%$) and argue the direction of the error, and close by explaining
why a study with \emph{no bias at all} still cannot establish causation.

\textbf{Extension (optional).} Students find a real headline claiming causation,
name the explanatory and response variables, say whether the evidence came from
watching or assigning, name a lurking variable, and rewrite the headline so it
claims only what the study supports. Collect these --- with the 1.2, 1.3, and
1.4 extensions they are nearly a full proposal for the Lesson 1.8 project.

\textbf{Preview of Lesson 1.6.} An observational study cannot reach causation
because the groups build themselves. So build them instead: treatment and
control, assigned at random, with the four principles --- comparison,
randomization, replication, control (PS.DC.3a--b).

\textbf{Connections \& Big Ideas.} \textit{Unit 1 core idea:} a conclusion is
only as trustworthy as the study that produced it. \textit{Standards covered:}
PS.DC.2d (with PS.DC.1a--b carried forward in the cycle and the conclusion
items). \textit{Spirals forward to:} experimental design (1.6, PS.DC.3a--b),
comparing experiments with observational studies and choosing a collection
method (1.7, PS.DC.3c--e), the class-survey project (1.8, PS.DC.2d), two-way
tables (3.1), and --- directly --- association versus causation with
scatterplots and regression in Unit 5 (PS.DS.5, PS.DS.6). \textit{Reaches back
to:} Lesson 1.1's statistical cycle, Lesson 1.3's sampling plan, and Lesson
1.4's bias, which this lesson removes on purpose so that what remains is
visible.
\end{skillbox}

\begin{teachernote}[Warm-Up]
    Five minutes, silent, timed. All three items are Algebra 1 percent work and
    the class has done the identical moves in 1.2 and 1.4, so this should be
    near-universal.

    The one thing to watch is the denominators. Item 1 divides by $80$ and item
    2 divides by $120$ --- different groups, different wholes. Item 3(b) then
    asks for the pooled rate, and the wrong answer is $(70 + 45) \div 2 =
    57.5\%$, which averages two unequal groups as if they were the same size.
    The right path is $110 \div 200 = 55\%$. Catch that one at the collection,
    because section 3 of the notes and the entire homework depend on students
    keeping two denominators straight.

    Leave $70\%$, $45\%$, and $25$ points on the board. The hook needs them
    thirty seconds after the warm-up is collected.
\end{teachernote}

\begin{teachernote}[Guided Notes]
    Pace: section 1 in 7 minutes, section 2 in 8, section 3 in 8, section 4 in
    14, section 5 in 10, guided practice in 8. \textbf{Section 4 is the lesson};
    sections 1 and 2 are definition and review and should move fast.

    Section 2 is where PS.DC.2d is actually met --- the standard asks students
    to \emph{use the statistical cycle to plan and conduct an observational
    study}, and this table is that plan. Do not skip it because it looks like
    review of 1.1. The Collect row should come back word-for-word from Lesson
    1.3 (a random sample of $200$ from the full membership list), and it is
    worth saying out loud that the plan is 1.3's and the bias check is 1.4's,
    so everything the class already knows is still standing.

    Section 4 fails if you reveal the retirement row too early. Give story 1,
    then ask the class to invent story 2 --- the reversed arrow --- before you
    offer it. Somebody reliably says ``maybe they get up early because they
    sleep well,'' and the lesson is much stronger when it comes from the room.
    Only then show $75\%$ against $15\%$.

    If time runs short, compress section 5 (the homework re-teaches it with a
    four-row table and two written items) --- never section 4.
\end{teachernote}

\begin{teachernote}[Group Activity]
    Groups of three, 15--18 minutes, tiers assigned rather than chosen. Read the
    headline box aloud, especially the principal's memo. The activity is built
    so that the obvious reading of the data is not just unsupported but exactly
    backwards, and groups need to feel the pull of the wrong answer first.

    Tier R item 3 and item 4 have different denominators ($60$ and $90$) on
    purpose. A group that divides $39$ by $150$ has merged the two groups; catch
    it at the table, since Tier A item 1 then \emph{does} want the pooled $150$
    and the two must stay distinct.

    Tier A item 3 is the writing item and it is where the banned verbs go. If a
    group writes ``the Study Center hurts grades,'' hand it back with the verb
    circled --- do not explain, just circle. Most groups fix it themselves on
    the second pass.

    Tier E item 1 is the best item on the page: $75\%$ of users had already
    failed a class, against $10\%$ of non-users. Item 2 then asks for the
    \emph{direction}, and the answer --- the center looks worse than it is ---
    requires chaining that fact to the comparison. Pull it to the whole class if
    two or more groups reach it. Item 3 is a first taste of Lesson 1.6: option
    (b) is a randomized experiment in everything but name, and the cost line
    (you must deny half the students help) is the ethical objection that recurs
    all through the next lesson. Insist on both halves of each line.
\end{teachernote}

\begin{teachernote}[Exit Ticket]
    Five minutes, notes closed, collected at the door. Item 1 should be near
    universal; if it is not, section 1 did not land and it is worth three
    minutes at the start of Lesson 1.6.

    Item 2 is the arithmetic check and the denominators are $150$ and $600$ ---
    the same trap as the warm-up, so a student who missed it twice needs the
    reteach more than the concept.

    Grade item 3 first and only item 3 if time is short. Full credit needs both
    halves: \emph{no}, and a concrete alternative explanation tied to this
    context. Watch for the answer that says ``correlation is not causation'' and
    stops --- it is a slogan, not a justification, and those students will
    abandon it the first time a causal story sounds plausible. Write the missing
    half on the paper: \emph{name one other reason band students might already
    have higher grades.}
\end{teachernote}

\boxguard[26]
\begin{teachernote}[Homework]
    Assign all eight items; about $30$ minutes. Items 4, 5, 7(b), and 8 cannot
    be answered by computing anything --- flag them when assigning, because
    students who treat this as a calculation sheet will skip exactly those.

    Items 2 and 3 are the arithmetic spine and are designed to be done in order:
    $80\%$, $32\%$, the $48$-point gap, then the pooled $50\%$ and $600$. The
    predictable error is $120/1200$ or averaging $80$ and $32$; both come from
    losing track of which whole is which.

    Item 6's four rows escalate. Ice cream and sunburn is the giveaway (hot
    weather); shoe size and reading level is the classic (age); the online
    catalog row is the hardest because the lurking variable is a habit rather
    than a thing, and ``the kind of person who plans ahead'' is full credit.

    Item 8 is the closer and the whole point of putting this lesson after 1.4.
    The sample was random, everyone answered, every count is right --- so the
    answer cannot be ``the study was biased.'' It has to be that the card
    holders sorted \emph{themselves}, and no sampling method fixes that.
    Students who answer ``the sample was too small'' or ``it was biased'' are
    reaching for the previous lesson's tool; write the correction rather than
    marking it, because the same reflex will cost them on the unit test.

    The \textbf{remindbox} hands off to Lesson 1.6 and names all four principles
    of experimental design. Do not teach them here.
\end{teachernote}
