\boxguard[30]
\begin{notesbox}{1. Who Chose the Sample --- Chance, or Convenience?}
\small
Every study you have seen so far had a sample. None of them said \emph{how} the
sample was picked. That decision is what this lesson is about.

\vspace{2pt}
The \textbf{sampling frame} is the list of individuals you can actually choose
from. Once you have a frame, there are two very different ways to choose:

\vspace{2pt}
In a \textbf{probability sample}, \textbf{chance} does the choosing --- every
individual on the frame has a known chance of being picked. In a
\textbf{convenience sample}, the researcher simply takes whoever is easiest to
reach.

\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.4}
\begin{tabularx}{0.98\linewidth}{@{} X p{3.1cm} @{}}
\toprule
\textbf{How the sample was taken} & \textbf{Who chose it?} \\
\midrule
A staff member asks the members who arrive Tuesday evening.
  & \blank{2.7cm} \\
All $900$ students are numbered, and a computer picks $60$ numbers.
  & \blank{2.7cm} \\
A teacher surveys her own third-period class.
  & \blank{2.7cm} \\
The $36$ homeroom numbers go in a hat and $3$ are drawn.
  & \blank{2.7cm} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}

\vspace{3pt}
All four techniques in today's lesson are \textbf{probability samples} --- chance
does the choosing in every one of them. What makes them different is \emph{what}
chance gets to choose from.

\vspace{2pt}
\textit{A convenience sample is not a technique you may use and defend. It is the
thing the other four exist to replace.}
\end{notesbox}

\boxguard
\begin{notesbox}{2. Simple Random Sample (SRS)}
\small
In a \textbf{simple random sample} of size $n$, every individual has an equal
chance of being chosen --- and so does every possible group of $n$ individuals.

\vspace{2pt}
\textbf{How to take one:} number the frame $1$ to $N$; run a random number
generator; read numbers off, \emph{skipping} any that are out of range or already
used; stop when you have $n$ different individuals.

\vspace{3pt}
Riverbend's $900$ students are numbered $001$ through $900$. A random digit
generator returns this run of digits:

\begin{center}
\ttfamily\large 7 1 4 0 2 8 9 5 1 3 6 6
\end{center}

\vspace{2pt}
Read them in groups of three, then decide each group.

\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.4}
\begin{tabularx}{0.95\linewidth}{@{} c p{3.6cm} X @{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Digits} & \textbf{In range $001$--$900$?} & \textbf{Student chosen} \\
\midrule
714 & yes & student 714 \\
028 & \blank{2.2cm} & \blank{3.6cm} \\
951 & \blank{2.2cm} & \blank{3.6cm} \\
366 & \blank{2.2cm} & \blank{3.6cm} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}

\vspace{3pt}
Why read the digits three at a time? Because the largest number on the frame,
$900$, has \blank{1.6cm} digits.

\vspace{2pt}
\textit{An SRS is the fairest of the four, and the one the others are measured
against. Its price: you need a complete list of the population, and the
individuals it picks may be scattered anywhere.}
\end{notesbox}

\boxguard[30]
\begin{notesbox}{3. Stratified Sample --- Some From Every Group}
\small
Some populations split into groups whose members are \emph{alike inside} the
group and \emph{different between} groups. Those groups are called
\textbf{strata} (just one is a \emph{stratum}).

\vspace{2pt}
In a \textbf{stratified sample} you take a separate random sample from
\textbf{every} stratum, sized so each stratum keeps its share of the population.

\vspace{3pt}
The council wants $60$ of Riverbend's $900$ students, stratified by grade. The
sample is $60/900 = 1/15$ of the school, so take $1/15$ of each grade.

\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.4}
\begin{tabularx}{0.9\linewidth}{@{} l c X @{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Grade} & \textbf{Students} & \textbf{Number to survey} \\
\midrule
9th   & 270 & $270 \div 15 = 18$ \\
10th  & 240 & \blank{4.2cm} \\
11th  & 210 & \blank{4.2cm} \\
12th  & 180 & \blank{4.2cm} \\
\midrule
\textbf{Total} & \textbf{900} & \blank{4.2cm} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}

\vspace{3pt}
Show the work for the \textbf{10th grade} row:

\begin{work}
  \dfrac{60}{900} &= \dfrac{1}{15} \\
  \dfrac{1}{15} \times 240 &= 16 \text{ students}
\end{work}

\vspace{2pt}
\textbf{Why go to the trouble?} Ninth graders and twelfth graders do not feel the
same way about a late bus --- most seniors drive. Stratifying
\emph{guarantees} every grade is heard, in the right proportion. An SRS might, by
bad luck, pick hardly any seniors at all.
\end{notesbox}

\boxguard[26]
\begin{notesbox}{4. Systematic Sample --- Every $k$th One Down the List}
\small
If the population is already in a list, you can skip the $60$ random draws and
take every $k$th individual instead.

\vspace{2pt}
\textbf{How to take one:} put the population in a list of $N$; compute the
interval $k = N \div n$; randomly choose a starting point between $1$ and $k$;
then take every $k$th individual from there.

\vspace{3pt}
Riverbend's list has $N = 900$ names and the council wants $n = 60$.

\begin{work}
  k &= 900 \div 60 \\
    &= 15
\end{work}

\vspace{2pt}
The random start comes out at the $7$th name. Fill in the rest of the picks:

\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.4}
\begin{tabularx}{0.9\linewidth}{@{} l X @{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Pick} & \textbf{Position on the list} \\
\midrule
1st & 7 \\
2nd & \blank{2.4cm} \\
3rd & \blank{2.4cm} \\
4th & \blank{2.4cm} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}

\vspace{3pt}
Notice that only the \textbf{start} is random --- after that, the list decides.
That is still enough to make it a probability sample, \emph{as long as} the order
of the list has nothing to do with the interval.
\end{notesbox}

\boxguard[30]
\begin{notesbox}{5. Cluster Sample --- Everyone From a Few Groups}
\small
Sometimes there is no list of individuals at all, but the population already
comes in natural groups: homerooms, apartment buildings, city blocks, flights.
Those groups are called \textbf{clusters}, and each one is meant to be a small
mixed version of the whole population.

\vspace{2pt}
In a \textbf{cluster sample} you randomly choose a few whole clusters and collect
data from \textbf{everyone} in them.

\vspace{3pt}
Riverbend's $900$ students sit in $36$ homerooms of $25$. The council draws
$3$ homeroom numbers at random and surveys every student in those three rooms.
How many students is that?

\begin{work}
  3 \times 25 &= 75 \text{ students}
\end{work}

\vspace{3pt}
\textbf{Stratified and cluster are the two everybody mixes up.} Fill in the
contrast:

\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.45}
\begin{tabularx}{0.98\linewidth}{@{} p{3.5cm} X X @{}}
\toprule
 & \textbf{Stratified} & \textbf{Cluster} \\
\midrule
The groups are\dots & alike inside, different from each other
                    & each a small mix of the whole \\
You use\dots        & \blank{2.6cm} of the groups & \blank{2.6cm} of the groups \\
From each group you take\dots & \blank{2.6cm} & \blank{2.6cm} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}

\vspace{3pt}
\textit{Say it this way:} \textbf{stratified is some from every group; cluster is
everyone from a few groups.}

\vspace{2pt}
Cluster sampling is the cheapest of the four and the riskiest. If the clusters
really do differ from one another, three of them can miss the population badly.
\end{notesbox}
