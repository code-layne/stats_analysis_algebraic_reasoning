    \textbf{Primary Objective:} Students will describe and carry out the four
    probability sampling techniques --- simple random, stratified, systematic,
    and cluster --- computing a stratified allocation and a systematic interval,
    distinguishing a stratum from a cluster, and choosing the technique a given
    context calls for with a reason that names what the context allows.
\end{tcolorbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Priority Ideas \& Skills]{goldbox}
\begin{minipage}[t]{0.49\linewidth}\vspace{0pt}
    \textbf{Skills for this lesson}
    \begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.1em, itemsep=2pt, topsep=2pt]
      \item Identify the \textbf{sampling frame} and say whether chance or
            convenience chose a sample. \hfill \textit{PS.DC.2a}
      \item Describe and execute a \textbf{simple random sample} from numbered
            digits, skipping out-of-range and repeat values.
            \hfill \textit{PS.DC.2a; AFDA.DA.2b}
      \item Compute a \textbf{stratified} allocation proportional to the strata
            sizes. \hfill \textit{PS.DC.2a; AFDA.DA.2b}
      \item Compute a \textbf{systematic} interval $k = N \div n$ and list the
            selections from a random start. \hfill \textit{PS.DC.2a}
      \item Describe a \textbf{cluster} sample and count the individuals it
            reaches. \hfill \textit{PS.DC.2a; AFDA.DA.2b}
      \item \textbf{Choose} the best technique for a context and justify it from
            what the context allows. \hfill \textit{PS.DC.2b; AFDA.DA.2b}
    \end{itemize}
\end{minipage}
\hfill
\begin{minipage}[t]{0.47\linewidth}\vspace{0pt}
    \textbf{Key Understandings}
    \begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.1em, itemsep=2pt, topsep=2pt]
      \item \emph{The method is the study's weakest link.} Lesson 1.2 said a
            statistic estimates a parameter; whether it estimates it \emph{well}
            is decided here, before any data exist.
      \item In a probability sample, \textbf{chance} does the choosing. That is
            the single line separating all four techniques from a convenience
            sample.
      \item Nobody left off the \textbf{frame} can ever be sampled, however good
            the method is. The Tuesday-evening frame was one evening's arrivals.
      \item \textbf{Strata are alike inside; clusters are supposed to be a small
            mix of the whole.} That one sentence is the whole stratified/cluster
            distinction, and everything else follows from it.
      \item ``Best'' is not a property of a technique --- it is a property of a
            technique \emph{in a context}. What list exists, and which subgroups
            differ, decide it.
    \end{itemize}
\end{minipage}
\end{skillbox}

\boxguard[24]
\begin{skillbox}[{Vocabulary, Concepts, \& Theorems}]{greenbox}
\small
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.35}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{} >{\bfseries}p{3.1cm} X @{}}
Sampling frame & The list of individuals a study can actually choose from.
       Anyone off the frame cannot be sampled. \\
Probability sample & A sample in which chance does the choosing and every
       individual on the frame has a known chance of selection. \\
Simple random sample & Every individual --- and every group of $n$ individuals
       --- has an equal chance of being selected. \\
Stratified sample & The population is split into \emph{strata} whose members are
       alike; a random sample is drawn from \emph{every} stratum, in proportion. \\
Systematic sample & From an ordered list, take every $k$th individual after a
       random start, where $k = N \div n$. \\
Cluster sample & The population is split into \emph{clusters}; a few whole
       clusters are drawn at random and everyone in them is measured. \\
Convenience sample & A sample of whoever is easiest to reach --- the researcher,
       not chance, does the choosing. \\
Stratum / cluster & A stratum is alike \emph{inside} and differs from other
       strata; a cluster is meant to be a small mix of the whole population. \\
\end{tabularx}
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Hook]{frost}
    Put last night's homework sentence back on the board: \textit{``$36\%$ of
    Harbor Point's members mainly swim laps''} --- and under it, \textbf{75
    members, Tuesday evening}. \textbf{Ask: who at that pool had no chance of
    being asked?} Collect names until somebody says \emph{morning swimmers}, and
    then keep going --- weekend families, anyone who works evenings, seniors who
    swim at $10$~a.m. Write them in a column headed \textbf{never had a chance}.
    That column is the lesson: a sample is not just a size, it is a
    \emph{procedure}, and this one's procedure quietly deleted half the
    membership before a single question was asked. Then ask the turn:
    \textbf{whose fault is it --- the staff member's, or the schedule's?} The
    answer students need is that the \emph{evening} chose the sample. Today is
    about handing that job to chance instead.
\end{skillbox}

\boxguard
\begin{skillbox}[Lesson]{frost}
\begin{multicols}{2}
\textbf{1. Chance, or convenience.} Frame first, then the two ways to choose.
The four-row table is fast; the only row that argues is the teacher surveying her
own third period, which many students read as random because she did not pick
favorites. Press: \emph{could a fourth-period student have been chosen?} No.
Close with the line that organizes the whole lesson --- all four techniques are
probability samples, and they differ only in \emph{what chance gets to choose
from}.

\textbf{2. Simple random sample.} Run the digit strip live. Read
\texttt{714 028 951 366} across the board and stop at $951$: \textbf{ask what to
do with it} before telling them. The skip is the whole skill. Then the
three-digit question --- why groups of three? --- which students answer
correctly and cannot always explain. Name the price of an SRS out loud: you need
a complete list, and your $60$ students end up scattered across the building.

\textbf{3. Stratified.} The warm-up already computed $18$; start there and ask
what they were doing without knowing it. Run the $1/15$ table --- the arithmetic
is one division per row and should take three minutes. Spend the time instead on
\emph{why}: seniors drive, so seniors do not care about a late bus. Stratifying
\textbf{guarantees} the grades are heard; an SRS only makes it likely.

\textbf{4. Systematic.} $k = 900 \div 60 = 15$, random start $7$, then count on.
Ask the sharp question: \textbf{what is actually random here?} Only the start.
Then plant the trap the activity pays off --- a list whose order lines up with
the interval.

\textbf{5. Cluster --- and the pivot.} Homerooms. Draw three, survey all $75$.
Then the contrast table, which is the most important block in the notes: some
from every group versus everyone from a few groups. Make the class say the
one-liner out loud before they write it. Close by naming cluster as the cheapest
and riskiest of the four --- it is the only one whose failure mode students meet
again in the activity.

\textbf{Guided practice.} Three contexts, three techniques, and a written reason
for each. This is PS.DC.2b and it is the part that will not be finished; assign
the pool row for homework if time runs out.
\end{multicols}
\end{skillbox}

\boxguard
\begin{skillbox}[Explicit Instruction: Choosing a Technique for a Context]{frost}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{} p{0.46\linewidth} X @{}}
\begin{minipage}[t]{\linewidth}\vspace{0pt}
\textbf{Steps --- give these verbatim}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.4em, itemsep=2pt, topsep=2pt]
  \item \textbf{What list exists?} A list of individuals, a list of groups, or
        no list at all?
  \item \textbf{Is there a subgroup that must be heard?} If subgroups differ on
        the variable of interest, that points to \textbf{stratified}.
  \item \textbf{Is the population spread out, or already gathered in groups?}
        That points to \textbf{cluster}.
  \item \textbf{Is the population already in order --- a line, a list, a
        conveyor?} That points to \textbf{systematic}.
  \item \textbf{None of the above?} Take the \textbf{simple random sample}. It
        is the default, not the fallback.
  \item Write the reason as \emph{``\dots because the context gives me
        \underline{\hspace{1.4cm}}.''} A technique named with no reason is not
        an answer.
\end{enumerate}
\end{minipage}
&
\begin{minipage}[t]{\linewidth}\vspace{0pt}
\textbf{Worked example --- ``A county wants opinions from $1{,}200$ households
over $400$ square miles. There is no list of residents, only a map of blocks.''}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.1em, itemsep=2pt, topsep=2pt]
  \item[1.] What list exists? Only a list of \emph{blocks} --- groups, not
        individuals.
  \item[2.] Any subgroup that must be heard? None is named, so stratified is not
        forced.
  \item[3.] Spread out over $400$ square miles and already gathered into blocks
        --- that is the cluster signal.
  \item[4.] Not in any order, so systematic does not apply.
  \item[5.] An SRS is impossible here: you cannot number households you have no
        list of.
  \item[6.] \textbf{Cluster}, \emph{because the context gives me a list of
        groups and no list of individuals.}
\end{itemize}
\textit{Contrast: give the county a complete resident roll and the same question
becomes an SRS --- the context changed, so the answer changed.}
\end{minipage}
\end{tabularx}
\end{skillbox}

\boxguard
\begin{skillbox}[Active Monitoring]{redbox}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em, itemsep=2pt, topsep=2pt]
  \item \textbf{Watch for ``random'' meaning ``unplanned.''} Students call the
        cafeteria-door sample random because nobody was chosen on purpose. Ask
        every time: \emph{who could not possibly have been picked?} If that list
        is non-empty, chance did not choose.
  \item \textbf{Watch for stratified and cluster swapping.} This is the error of
        the lesson. Diagnostic question: \emph{how many of the groups did you
        use?} All of them $\Rightarrow$ stratified. A few $\Rightarrow$ cluster.
        Do not accept the group names as evidence.
  \item \textbf{Watch for the interval computed as $n \div N$.} A student who
        writes $60 \div 900$ gets $0.0\overline{6}$ and moves on. Catch it by
        asking what the units of $k$ are --- names, not fractions.
  \item \textbf{Watch for ``skip it'' meaning ``stop.''} On the digit strip,
        some students discard $951$ and abandon the row. The generator keeps
        running; only that number is discarded.
  \item \textbf{Watch for a technique named with no reason} on the practice box
        and on activity Tier E. Half credit at most --- PS.DC.2b is the
        justification, not the label.
  \item \textbf{Cold-call prompts:} ``Who chose that sample?'' ``What list do
        they have?'' ``How many of the groups did you use?'' ``Who could never
        have been picked?''
\end{itemize}
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Individual Work \& Assessment]{redbox}
\textbf{Exit ticket (3 items, no notes).} One context: Bayside Middle School,
$750$ students in three grades, surveying $30$.
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.4em, itemsep=2pt, topsep=2pt]
  \item Name the technique in three plans --- one cluster, one stratified, one
        convenience.
  \item Compute $k = 750 \div 30 = 25$ and list the next two selections from a
        random start of $6$ ($31$ and $56$).
  \item \textbf{Conceptual / justification check:} which technique
        \emph{guarantees} all three grades appear in proportion (stratified),
        and why does a perfectly fair SRS not guarantee it? Correct answer:
        fairness is a statement about each student's \emph{chance}, not about
        how the sample turns out --- a fair draw can still land mostly on one
        grade. Grade this one first.
\end{enumerate}
\textbf{Using the results:} sort into three piles --- (a) said the SRS is fair
but makes no promise about the outcome, (b) said an SRS \emph{does} guarantee
proportion because it is fair, (c) answered ``stratified'' to both parts without
addressing chance. Pile (b) is the productive one and will be the largest; it is
the same confusion between a procedure and its outcome that Unit 7 formalizes,
so name it rather than marking it wrong.
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Reinforcement \& Extension]{goldbox}
\textbf{Homework --- Harbor Point Community Pool, done properly.} The board
throws out the Tuesday-evening study and the student plans the replacement:
name what the original was, run an SRS off generator output (skipping one
out-of-range and one repeat value), allocate a stratified sample by membership
type, compute a systematic interval, count a cluster sample of swim sessions,
match four outside studies to their techniques, choose a plan for a stated board
requirement and justify it, and close with a words-only item on why a random
cluster draw can still miss.

\textbf{Extension (optional).} Students pick a population they could really
study and plan \emph{two} samples of the same size by different techniques, then
say which they would run and why. Collect these --- with the Lesson 1.2
extension they are most of a proposal for the Lesson 1.8 class-survey project.

\textbf{Preview of Lesson 1.4.} Every technique today assumed the people chosen
actually answer, and answer honestly, and understood the question the same way.
Next class drops all three assumptions: undercoverage, nonresponse, response
bias, and question wording --- PS.DC.2c and AFDA.DA.2e--g.

\textbf{Connections \& Big Ideas.} \textit{Unit 1 core idea:} a conclusion is
only as trustworthy as the study that produced it. \textit{Standards covered:}
PS.DC.2a, PS.DC.2b; AFDA.DA.2b. \textit{Spirals forward to:} bias
(1.4, PS.DC.2c), observational studies (1.5, PS.DC.2d), randomization in
experimental design (1.6, PS.DC.3a--b), the full-cycle project (1.8), and ---
directly --- sampling distributions and margin of error (7.1--7.2).
\textit{Reaches back to:} Lesson 1.2's population/sample and sampling
variability, and Algebra 1 proportional reasoning.
\end{skillbox}

\begin{teachernote}[Warm-Up]
    Five minutes, silent, timed. All three items are Algebra 1 arithmetic and
    should be near-universal --- say so when you assign it, because item 3 looks
    unfamiliar and is not.

    Item 3 is the one to read while collecting. It is a systematic sample with
    the name removed: $900 \div 60 = 15$, start at $7$, next are $22$ and $37$.
    Students who write $22$ and $37$ have already done section 4 of the notes.
    Students who write $8$ and $9$ read ``next'' as ``the next name'' --- that is
    the misread to fix at the board, in ten seconds, before the notes begin.

    Do \emph{not} name any of the four techniques here. The warm-up is
    deliberately wordless about them; the notes name what the class has already
    computed.
\end{teachernote}

\begin{teachernote}[Guided Notes]
    Pace: section 1 in 7 minutes, section 2 in 10, section 3 in 8, section 4 in
    7, section 5 in 12, guided practice in 8. \textbf{Section 5 is the lesson.}
    Sections 2--4 are procedures students execute correctly on the first try;
    the stratified/cluster distinction is the one that does not stick without
    the contrast table and the spoken one-liner.

    Section 2's digit strip must be run live, not read. Stop at $951$ and take a
    vote on what to do with it before saying anything. The skip is the only
    non-obvious move in an SRS.

    Section 3 is arithmetically trivial and conceptually the opposite. Budget the
    saved minutes on the word \emph{guarantees} --- stratified guarantees each
    grade is heard, an SRS only makes it likely. That contrast returns as exit
    ticket item 3 and is the hardest idea in the lesson.

    If you are short on time, compress section 4 (the warm-up already did the
    arithmetic) --- never section 5 or the guided practice.
\end{teachernote}

\begin{teachernote}[Group Activity]
    Groups of three, 15--18 minutes, tiers assigned rather than chosen. The
    Cedar Ridge layout in the headline box is load-bearing --- buildings $1$--$6$
    are studios, $7$--$20$ one-bedrooms, $21$--$30$ two-bedrooms --- and Tier E
    item 2 does not work if groups skim past it. Read it aloud when you assign.

    Tier R item 2 row 4 (``every $10$th one after it'') is where groups that
    still have systematic and simple random fused will show it. Circulate there
    first.

    Tier A item 3 is the periodicity trap and the best item on the page: with
    $k = 20$ on a list of $20$-household buildings, a start of $1$ selects
    household \#1 of all $30$ buildings --- every corner unit, every double
    parking space, in a survey about parking. Groups that get it should present
    it.

    Tier E item 2 is the payoff for section 5. Cedar Ridge's buildings each hold
    one unit type, so a building is a \emph{stratum wearing a cluster's clothes}.
    Pull this to the whole class if two or more groups reach it --- it is worth
    the last five minutes of the period.
\end{teachernote}

\begin{teachernote}[Exit Ticket]
    Five minutes, notes closed, collected at the door. Item 1 should be near
    universal; if the cluster row is widely missed, the stratified/cluster
    contrast did not land and needs two minutes at the start of Lesson 1.4.

    Item 2(a) catches the inverted interval --- a student who writes
    $30 \div 750$ has the ratio upside down. Item 2(b) catches the student who
    adds $1$ instead of $k$.

    Grade item 3(b) first and only 3(b) if time is short. ``Stratified'' with no
    explanation is half credit; the reason --- a fair draw can still come out
    lopsided, because fairness is about each student's chance, not about the
    result --- is what Lesson 1.4 and all of Unit 7 build on. Watch for
    ``because an SRS is not fair,'' which inverts the point entirely and is worth
    correcting in writing rather than with a mark.
\end{teachernote}

\begin{teachernote}[Homework]
    Assign all eight items; about $30$ minutes. Items 7 and 8 cannot be answered
    by computing anything --- flag both when assigning, because students who
    treat this as a calculation sheet will skip exactly those two.

    Item 2's table has two different reasons for discarding a number: $1587$ is
    out of range and the second $0412$ is a repeat. Students who write ``no'' in
    both rows without distinguishing them have missed half the skill.

    Item 8 is the transfer item and closes the arc the lesson opened with. The
    sessions were drawn \emph{at random} --- so the answer cannot be ``they
    picked badly.'' It has to be that a swim session is not a small mix of the
    membership: lap swimmers fill lap hours, so four clusters can all lean the
    same way and leave families and seniors out. Students who reach for
    ``the sample was too small'' have the wrong lever; ask them what would happen
    if the four sessions held $500$ people.

    The \textbf{remindbox} hands off to Lesson 1.4 deliberately: every technique
    here assumed the chosen individuals answer, answer honestly, and read the
    question the same way. Do not open that here.
\end{teachernote}
