    \textbf{Primary Objective:} Students will name the population and the sample
    in a study, label a given number as a parameter or a statistic, explain the
    real-world constraints that make a census impractical, explain why two honest
    samples give two different statistics for one unchanging parameter, and judge
    what a sample does --- and does not --- allow a report to claim.
\end{tcolorbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Priority Ideas \& Skills]{goldbox}
\begin{minipage}[t]{0.49\linewidth}\vspace{0pt}
    \textbf{Skills for this lesson}
    \begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.1em, itemsep=2pt, topsep=2pt]
      \item Identify the \textbf{population}, the \textbf{sample}, and the
            \textbf{sample size} in a described study. \hfill \textit{PS.DC.1d}
      \item Distinguish a \textbf{parameter} from a \textbf{statistic}, and say
            which of the two is known. \hfill \textit{PS.DC.1d}
      \item Define a \textbf{census} and explain why it is rarely available.
            \hfill \textit{PS.DC.1d--e}
      \item Name the \textbf{constraint} --- time, cost, access, destruction,
            change --- that forces sampling in a given context.
            \hfill \textit{PS.DC.1e}
      \item Explain \textbf{sampling variability}: one parameter, many
            statistics. \hfill \textit{PS.DC.1e}
      \item Judge whether a written claim stays inside what the sample supports.
            \hfill \textit{PS.DC.1b, e}
    \end{itemize}
\end{minipage}
\hfill
\begin{minipage}[t]{0.47\linewidth}\vspace{0pt}
    \textbf{Key Understandings}
    \begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.1em, itemsep=2pt, topsep=2pt]
      \item \emph{The population is decided by the question, not by who happens
            to be available.} Naming it is a stage-1 act; the sample is chosen
            out of it afterwards.
      \item The number you \textbf{want} (the parameter) is almost never the
            number you \textbf{have} (the statistic). Everything after this
            lesson is about closing that gap.
      \item A statistic is not the parameter with error in it --- it is a
            \emph{different number} that varies by design. Two honest surveys
            disagreeing is normal, not evidence of a mistake.
      \item Constraints are not excuses. They are why sampling exists, and they
            are part of describing a study honestly.
      \item A conclusion may describe only the population the sample was drawn
            from --- change the population and the same statistic estimates a
            different parameter.
    \end{itemize}
\end{minipage}
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[{Vocabulary, Concepts, \& Theorems}]{greenbox}
\small
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.35}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{} >{\bfseries}p{3.1cm} X @{}}
Population & The entire group the question is about --- every individual you
       would like the conclusion to describe. \\
Sample & The part of the population data were actually collected from. \\
Sample size & How many individuals are in the sample. \\
Census & A study that collects data from every individual in the population. \\
Parameter & A number that describes the population. Usually unknown. \\
Statistic & A number that describes the sample; computed from collected data. \\
Constraint & A real-world limit on how a study can be run --- time, cost,
       access, destruction, or a changing population. \\
Sampling variability & The fact that a statistic changes from one sample to the
       next even when nothing about the population changes. \\
Estimate & Using a statistic as the best available stand-in for a parameter.
       \emph{(Made precise in Unit 7.)} \\
\end{tabularx}
\end{skillbox}

\boxguard
\begin{skillbox}[Hook]{frost}
    Put the manager's sentence on the board: \textit{``$52\%$ of our Saturday
    shoppers drive to the market.''} Under it write the two numbers from the
    warm-up: \textbf{50} and \textbf{800}.
    \textbf{Ask: she never asked 800 people. Is she allowed to write that
    sentence?} Take the argument --- there will be one. Then turn it:
    \textbf{why would she not just ask all 800?} Collect reasons on the board
    (too slow, they leave, nobody would answer, no money) and leave them there;
    those are the constraints you will name in section 3, and students will have
    supplied them themselves. Close the hook by pointing at $52\%$ and asking
    what number she \emph{actually wanted} to know. Nobody knows it. That gap is
    today's lesson.
\end{skillbox}

\boxguard
\begin{skillbox}[Lesson]{frost}
\begin{multicols}{2}
\textbf{1. Two groups.} Population, sample, sample size --- on the Lakeside study
they already know. Run the three-row table; the cereal-plant row is the one that
breaks the ``population $=$ people'' assumption, so do not skip it.
\textbf{Ask: can you name the sample before you name the population?} No --- the
sample is chosen \emph{out of} the population, so the population comes first.
Then define \textbf{census} and ask for an example. Most classes land on the U.S.
Census, and that is nearly the whole list.

\textbf{2. Parameter and statistic.} Give the p/p and s/s memory hook, then run
the four-row table. Row 4 (district records, $61\%$ of all $900$) is the one that
matters: it is a percent, like row 1, and it is still a parameter --- because of
\emph{who it describes}, not how it looks. Close with the two closing blanks:
you know the statistic, you want the parameter. Say plainly that Units 7 and 8
are entirely about that gap. The notation line ($\mu$, $\bar{x}$, $p$, $\hat p$)
is a preview only --- read it aloud, do not drill it.

\textbf{3. Constraints.} This is PS.DC.1e and it is easy to under-teach as a list.
Point back at the hook board: the class already generated these. Name the five,
then run the matching table. The cereal box (destruction) and the changing
Saturday crowd (change) are the two students have never considered.

\textbf{4. Sampling variability --- the pivot.} Ana, Ben, and Cleo asked three
different sets of $50$ shoppers on the \emph{same} morning and got $52\%$, $48\%$,
$58\%$. Compute Cleo's row in the work block, then ask the two counting
questions: \textbf{three statistics, one parameter.} Ask outright: \emph{which
one of them is wrong?} None. Let the class sit in that. This is the idea Unit 7
is built on.

\textbf{5. What a sample lets you say.} Fill the report sentence, then judge the
three claims. ``Exactly $416$'' is the subtle one --- the arithmetic is right and
the word \emph{exactly} is what fails. The Lakeside-residents claim changes the
population out from under the statistic.
\end{multicols}
\end{skillbox}

\boxguard
\begin{skillbox}[Explicit Instruction: Labeling a Number Parameter or Statistic]{frost}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{} p{0.46\linewidth} X @{}}
\begin{minipage}[t]{\linewidth}\vspace{0pt}
\textbf{Steps --- give these verbatim}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.4em, itemsep=2pt, topsep=2pt]
  \item Read the number and ask: \textbf{who does it describe?} Underline that
        group in the sentence.
  \item Is that group \textbf{everybody the question is about}, or only
        \textbf{the ones who were measured}?
  \item Everybody $\Rightarrow$ \textbf{parameter}. Only the measured ones
        $\Rightarrow$ \textbf{statistic}.
  \item Check yourself: could you compute it from the collected data? If yes,
        it is a statistic.
  \item Ignore what the number \emph{looks} like. A percent, a mean, and a count
        can each be either one.
\end{enumerate}
\end{minipage}
&
\begin{minipage}[t]{\linewidth}\vspace{0pt}
\textbf{Worked example --- ``$61\%$ of Riverbend's $900$ students ride a bus.''}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.1em, itemsep=2pt, topsep=2pt]
  \item[1.] Who does it describe? \emph{Riverbend's $900$ students} --- underline
        it.
  \item[2.] That is everybody the question is about, not a measured subset.
  \item[3.] Therefore a \textbf{parameter}.
  \item[4.] Could you get it from the $45$ surveys? No --- it came from district
        records covering all $900$.
  \item[5.] It is a percent, exactly like the $52\%$ statistic. The shape told
        you nothing; the group told you everything.
\end{itemize}
\textit{Contrast: ``the $8$ shoppers asked traveled a mean of $4$ miles'' ---
a mean, but it describes only the measured $8$, so it is a statistic.}
\end{minipage}
\end{tabularx}
\end{skillbox}

\boxguard
\begin{skillbox}[Active Monitoring]{redbox}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em, itemsep=2pt, topsep=2pt]
  \item \textbf{Watch for ``big number $=$ parameter.''} Students decide by size:
        $1{,}500$ must be the parameter, $75$ must be the statistic. Break it
        with a small population (Ashford's $400$) or a large sample. Force the
        question \emph{who does it describe?} every time.
  \item \textbf{Watch for percent $=$ statistic.} A percent computed from
        district records for all $900$ students is a parameter. Row 4 of the
        notes table exists for this; check it as you circulate.
  \item \textbf{Watch for ``one of them counted wrong.''} On the Ana/Ben/Cleo
        item, many students will assume an error. Ask: \emph{did they survey the
        same 50 people?} No. Then no count is wrong.
  \item \textbf{Watch for the population drifting.} On the activity's Tier E
        item 2, students say ``both are $35\%$, so same parameter.'' Ask them to
        finish ``$35\%$ of \underline{\hspace{1.6cm}}'' out loud for each library.
  \item \textbf{Cold-call prompts:} ``Who does that number describe?''
        ``Which one do you actually know?'' ``Name the constraint.''
        ``How many parameters are there?''
\end{itemize}
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Group Work \& Differentiation]{redbox}
\small
\begin{multicols}{3}
\textbf{Tier R --- Remediate}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1em, itemsep=1pt, topsep=2pt]
  \item Name population, sample, and sample size for one described survey.
  \item Complete a frequency table with one row worked as a model.
  \item Label two numbers parameter or statistic; scale a percent up and write
        the conclusion sentence.
\end{itemize}

\columnbreak
\textbf{Tier A --- Approaching Proficiency}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1em, itemsep=1pt, topsep=2pt]
  \item Match three situations to the constraint that blocks a census.
  \item Two samples, two statistics ($35\%$ and $45\%$) --- explain the
        difference and count the parameters.
  \item Compute a mean and justify labeling it a statistic.
\end{itemize}

\columnbreak
\textbf{Tier E --- Extension}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1em, itemsep=1pt, topsep=2pt]
  \item Find \emph{two} separate overclaims in one printed sentence.
  \item Two libraries, equal statistics, different parameters --- explain why.
  \item Plan a study for a mean: population, sample, statistic, parameter,
        constraint.
\end{itemize}
\end{multicols}
\end{skillbox}

\boxguard
\begin{skillbox}[Individual Work \& Assessment]{redbox}
\textbf{Exit ticket (3 items, no notes).} One context: Westfield High School,
$800$ students, $40$ asked in one lunch period, $65\%$ said cafeteria.
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.4em, itemsep=2pt, topsep=2pt]
  \item Name the population, the sample, and the sample size.
  \item Label three numbers parameter or statistic --- including the third,
        \emph{the true percent for all $800$}, which no one has computed and
        which is still a parameter.
  \item \textbf{Conceptual / justification check:} name one constraint that
        blocked a census, then explain whether a second survey returning $60\%$
        means one of the two is wrong. Correct answer: \emph{neither} --- a
        statistic varies from sample to sample, and both estimate the same single
        parameter. Grade this one first.
\end{enumerate}
\textbf{Using the results:} sort into three piles --- (a) said neither is wrong
and named sampling variability, (b) said one must be wrong or that the truth is
``somewhere in between'' without saying why, (c) recomputed something. Pile (b)
is the teachable one and will be large; it is also exactly the intuition Unit 7
formalizes, so name it now rather than correcting it as an error.
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Reinforcement \& Extension]{goldbox}
\textbf{Homework --- Harbor Point Community Pool.} A fresh context with the same
spine: name the population and sample, complete a frequency table with relative
frequencies, scale $36\%$ up to $1{,}500$ members, compute and label a mean,
sort three numbers into parameter and statistic, match four situations to their
constraints, explain why a second sample gave $40\%$, and close with a
correct-arithmetic / wrong-conclusion item that has to be answered in words.

\textbf{Extension (optional).} Students choose a group they belong to and plan a
study for it: population, realistic sample, the statistic they would compute, the
parameter it estimates, and the one constraint that blocks a census. Collect
these --- they feed the Lesson 1.8 class-survey project.

\textbf{Preview of Lesson 1.3.} The pool staff surveyed whoever arrived on a
Tuesday evening, and lap swimmers keep evening hours. Today's lesson says a
statistic estimates a parameter; next class asks \emph{which} samples are worth
trusting, and names the four techniques --- simple random, stratified,
systematic, cluster.

\textbf{Connections \& Big Ideas.} \textit{Unit 1 core idea:} a conclusion is only
as trustworthy as the study that produced it. \textit{Standards covered:}
PS.DC.1d, PS.DC.1e (with PS.DC.1b carried forward in the conclusion items).
\textit{Spirals forward to:} sampling techniques (1.3, PS.DC.2a--b), bias
(1.4, PS.DC.2c), the full-cycle project (1.8), and --- directly --- sampling
distributions and margin of error (7.1--7.2, PS.IS.1a, d).
\textit{Reaches back to:} Lesson 1.1's data cycle and conclusion-in-context, and
Algebra 1 percent and proportional reasoning.
\end{skillbox}

\begin{teachernote}[Warm-Up]
    Five minutes, silent, timed. Items 1 and 2 are last night's homework
    arithmetic on purpose --- say so, so nobody thinks they are being retested.
    Item 3 is the one to read while collecting: students who write $50$ and $800$
    in the right rows have already made today's distinction without the
    vocabulary, and the notes can move fast. Students who put $416$ in a row have
    confused an estimate with a group and will need section 1 slowly. Do
    \emph{not} supply the words \emph{sample} and \emph{population} here --- the
    warm-up is deliberately wordless about them.
\end{teachernote}

\begin{teachernote}[Guided Notes]
    Pace: section 1 in 8 minutes, section 2 in 12, section 3 in 8, section 4 in
    10, section 5 in 7. \textbf{Section 4 is the lesson.} Sections 1--3 are
    vocabulary students will absorb quickly; the idea that three honest surveys
    can disagree and none be wrong is the one that does not land on its own.

    Section 2's fourth table row --- $61\%$ from district records --- is the
    single most productive line in the notes. Every student who has decided
    ``percent means statistic'' gets caught there. Do not fix it for them; ask
    who the $61\%$ describes.

    The notation line at the end of section 2 is exposure only. Read it, say the
    symbols return in Units 4, 7, and 8, and move on --- do not assign or test
    them today.

    If you are short on time, compress section 3's matching table (the class
    generated those constraints during the hook) --- never section 4.
\end{teachernote}

\begin{teachernote}[Group Activity]
    Groups of three, 15 minutes, tiers assigned rather than chosen. Tier R item 1
    is where you will find out who is still guessing: circulate there first and
    make them point at the words in the scenario.

    Tier A item 2(b) is the same idea as notes section 4 in a new context; if a
    group writes ``the second librarian was more careful,'' send them back to the
    Ana/Ben/Cleo table rather than explaining.

    Tier E item 2 (Millbrook $1{,}200$ vs.\ Ashford $400$, both $35\%$) is the
    hardest item in the lesson and is worth pulling to the whole class if two or
    more groups get it. The answer students need to say out loud is that
    ``$35\%$'' is incomplete until you finish the sentence ``$35\%$ of
    \emph{whom}.''
\end{teachernote}

\begin{teachernote}[Exit Ticket]
    Five minutes, notes closed, collected at the door. Item 1 should be near
    universal. Item 2's third row is the discriminator --- a parameter nobody has
    computed is still a parameter; if that row is widely wrong, reopen it for two
    minutes before Lesson 1.3.

    Grade item 3(b) first and only 3(b) if time is short. ``No'' with no reason is
    half credit; the reason --- different samples, one parameter --- is what
    Lesson 1.3 and all of Unit 7 build on. Watch for the plausible-but-wrong
    ``the real answer is halfway between,'' which is worth naming as an
    interesting guess that Unit 7 will make precise rather than marking as
    nonsense.
\end{teachernote}

\begin{teachernote}[Homework]
    Assign all eight items; about 25 minutes. Items 7(b) and 8 cannot be answered
    by computing anything --- flag both when assigning, because students who
    treat this as a calculation sheet will skip exactly those two.

    Item 8 is the transfer item and has two distinct answers, not one said twice:
    the $36\%$ describes only the $75$ asked, \emph{and} nobody was ever asked
    about a second lane. Students who find one usually find the population error
    and miss the question error.

    The \textbf{remindbox} at the foot plants Lesson 1.3 deliberately --- the
    sample was whoever turned up on a Tuesday evening, which is a lap-swimmer's
    hour. Do not resolve that here; it is 1.3's opening and 1.4's bias work.
\end{teachernote}
