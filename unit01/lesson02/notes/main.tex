\boxguard[30]
\begin{notesbox}{1. Every Study Has Two Groups}
\small
The \textbf{population} is the entire group the question is about. The
\textbf{sample} is the part of the population you actually collect data from. The
number of individuals in the sample is the \textbf{sample size}.

\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.4}
\begin{tabularx}{0.98\linewidth}{@{} X p{3.3cm} p{2.8cm} c @{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Study} & \textbf{Population} & \textbf{Sample} &
\textbf{Sample size} \\
\midrule
The manager asks $50$ of the market's $800$ Saturday shoppers how they traveled.
  & \blank{2.9cm} & \blank{2.4cm} & \blank{1cm} \\
The principal asks $45$ of Riverbend's $900$ students how many hours a week they
work.
  & \blank{2.9cm} & \blank{2.4cm} & \blank{1cm} \\
A cereal plant weighs $30$ of the $12{,}000$ boxes it filled on Tuesday.
  & \blank{2.9cm} & \blank{2.4cm} & \blank{1cm} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}

\vspace{3pt}
Collecting data from \emph{every} individual in the population is called a
\blank{3cm}. The U.S. Census is one; almost nothing else you meet in this course
will be.

\vspace{2pt}
\textit{The sample is always chosen out of the population --- so before you can
name a sample, you have to know what the population is.}
\end{notesbox}

\boxguard
\begin{notesbox}{2. Two Kinds of Numbers: Parameter and Statistic}
\small
Both groups produce numbers, and the two kinds are not interchangeable.

\vspace{2pt}
A number that describes the \textbf{population} is a \blank{3cm}.\\
A number that describes the \textbf{sample} is a \blank{3cm}.

\vspace{2pt}
\textit{Memory hook:} \textbf{p}opulation goes with \textbf{p}arameter;
\textbf{s}ample goes with \textbf{s}tatistic.

\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.45}
\begin{tabularx}{0.98\linewidth}{@{} X p{2.6cm} p{2.6cm} @{}}
\toprule
\textbf{The number} & \textbf{Describes\dots} & \textbf{So it is a\dots} \\
\midrule
$52\%$ of the $50$ shoppers asked drove to the market.
  & \blank{2.2cm} & \blank{2.2cm} \\
The market had $800$ shoppers on Saturday morning.
  & \blank{2.2cm} & \blank{2.2cm} \\
The $8$ shoppers asked traveled a mean of $4$ miles.
  & \blank{2.2cm} & \blank{2.2cm} \\
District records show $61\%$ of Riverbend's $900$ students ride a bus.
  & \blank{2.2cm} & \blank{2.2cm} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}

\vspace{3pt}
Which one do you almost always \emph{know}? The \blank{2.6cm}.
Which one do you actually \emph{want}? The \blank{2.6cm}.

\vspace{2pt}
That gap is the whole reason this course exists: you use the number you have to
estimate the number you want.

\vspace{2pt}
{\small\textit{Notation you will meet later in the course: a population mean is
written $\mu$ and a sample mean $\bar{x}$; a population percent is written $p$
and a sample percent $\hat{p}$. You do not need these symbols today.}}
\end{notesbox}

\boxguard[26]
\begin{notesbox}{3. Constraints --- Why We Sample at All}
\small
A \textbf{constraint} is something in the real world that limits how a study can
be run. Constraints are the reason a census is usually off the table.

\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.3}
\begin{tabularx}{0.95\linewidth}{@{} p{3.4cm} X @{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Constraint} & \textbf{What it means} \\
\midrule
Time     & Reaching everyone would take longer than you have. \\
Cost     & Reaching everyone would cost more than the budget. \\
Access   & Some individuals cannot be found, reached, or made to answer. \\
Destruction & Measuring the individual ruins it, so you cannot measure them all. \\
Change   & The population keeps changing while you collect. \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}

\vspace{3pt}
Name the constraint that stands in the way in each situation.

\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.5}
\begin{tabularx}{0.97\linewidth}{@{} X p{3.2cm} @{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Situation} & \textbf{Constraint} \\
\midrule
To check a cereal box's weight you must open it, and $12{,}000$ were filled
Tuesday. & \blank{2.8cm} \\
A different set of shoppers comes to the market every Saturday.
  & \blank{2.8cm} \\
Surveying all $900$ Riverbend students would take three class periods nobody can
spare. & \blank{2.8cm} \\
Reaching every household in the county would mean mailing $40{,}000$ letters.
  & \blank{2.8cm} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}
\end{notesbox}

\boxguard
\begin{notesbox}{4. The Statistic Moves. The Parameter Does Not.}
\small
Three volunteers each asked a \emph{different} $50$ shoppers on the \emph{same}
Saturday morning. Here is how many in each group said they drove.

\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.4}
\begin{tabularx}{0.9\linewidth}{@{} l c X @{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Volunteer} & \textbf{Drove} & \textbf{Percent of that sample} \\
\midrule
Ana   & 26 & $26/50 = 0.52 = 52\%$ \\
Ben   & 24 & \blank{4.5cm} \\
Cleo  & 29 & \blank{4.5cm} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}

\vspace{3pt}
Show the work for \textbf{Cleo's} row:

\begin{work}
  \dfrac{29}{50} &= 0.58 \\
                 &= 58\%
\end{work}

\vspace{2pt}
How many different statistics did the three volunteers produce? \blank{1.4cm}

How many true percents are there for all $800$ Saturday shoppers? \blank{1.4cm}

\vspace{2pt}
This change from one sample to the next is called \textbf{sampling variability}.
It is not a mistake --- nobody miscounted. It is what samples do.

\vspace{2pt}
\textit{A statistic is an \textbf{estimate} of the parameter. A larger sample
usually lands closer to it, but no sample lands exactly on it every time.}
\end{notesbox}

\begin{practicebox}
\small
The Riverbend principal wants to know what fraction of the school's $900$ students
hold a part-time job. She surveys $45$ students; $18$ of them have one.

\textbf{(a)} What percent of the students she surveyed have a part-time job?

\begin{work}
  \dfrac{18}{45} &= 0.40 \\
                 &= 40\%
\end{work}

\textbf{(b)} Estimate how many of Riverbend's $900$ students have a part-time job.

\begin{work}
  0.40 \times 900 &= 360 \text{ students}
\end{work}

\textbf{(c)} Which of your two answers is a statistic, and which is an estimate of
a parameter?
\writelines{2}

\textbf{(d)} Name one constraint that kept the principal from asking all $900$
students.
\writelines{2}
\end{practicebox}
