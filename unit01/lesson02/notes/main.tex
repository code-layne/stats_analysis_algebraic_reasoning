\begin{notesbox}{1. Two Groups --- and the Two Kinds of Numbers They Produce}
\small
Last night's homework asked $50$ shoppers and then talked about all $800$. Those two groups
have names, and so do the numbers computed from them.
\wordbank{population \;\textbullet\; sample \;\textbullet\; parameter \;\textbullet\; statistic \;\textbullet\; census \;\textbullet\; who \;\textbullet\; all \;\textbullet\; 50 \;\textbullet\; 800}
The \textbf{population} is the entire group the question is about --- every individual you
would like the conclusion to describe. At Lakeside, that is all \blank{1.2cm} Saturday
shoppers. The \textbf{sample} is the part of the population you actually collected data from
--- the \blank{1.2cm} shoppers the manager asked. The number of individuals in the sample is
the \textbf{sample size}, $n = $ \blank{1.2cm}.

\vspace{3pt}
Name the two groups in each study --- \emph{the population first; the sample is chosen out of it.}
\nopagebreak

\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.4}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{} X p{3.3cm} p{2.8cm} c @{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Study} & \textbf{Population} & \textbf{Sample} & \textbf{Size} \\
\midrule
The manager asks $50$ of the market's $800$ Saturday shoppers how they traveled.
  & \blank{2.9cm} & \blank{2.4cm} & \blank{1cm} \\
The principal asks $45$ of Riverbend's $900$ students how many hours a week they work.
  & \blank{2.9cm} & \blank{2.4cm} & \blank{1cm} \\
A cereal plant weighs $30$ of the $12{,}000$ boxes it filled on Tuesday.
  & \blank{2.9cm} & \blank{2.4cm} & \blank{1cm} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}

\vspace{3pt}
Collecting data from \emph{every} individual in the population is called a \blank{2cm}. The
U.S. Census is one; almost nothing else you meet in this course will be.

\vspace{6pt}
\textbf{\textcolor{cerulean}{Parameter and Statistic.}}
Both groups produce numbers, and the two kinds are not interchangeable.

\vspace{3pt}
A number that describes the \textbf{population} is a \blank{2.4cm}. \quad
A number that describes the \textbf{sample} is a \blank{2.4cm}.

\vspace{2pt}
\textit{Memory hook:} \textbf{p}opulation--\textbf{p}arameter, \textbf{s}ample--\textbf{s}tatistic. To sort a number, ask: \textbf{who does it describe?}
\nopagebreak

\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.45}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{} X p{2.6cm} p{2.6cm} @{}}
\toprule
\textbf{The number} & \textbf{Describes\dots} & \textbf{So it is a\dots} \\
\midrule
$52\%$ of the $50$ shoppers asked drove to the market.
  & \blank{2.2cm} & \blank{2.2cm} \\
The market had $800$ shoppers on Saturday morning.
  & \blank{2.2cm} & \blank{2.2cm} \\
The $8$ shoppers asked traveled a mean of $4$ miles.
  & \blank{2.2cm} & \blank{2.2cm} \\
District records show $61\%$ of Riverbend's $900$ students ride a bus.
  & \blank{2.2cm} & \blank{2.2cm} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}

\vspace{4pt}
\begin{center}
\fcolorbox{redacc}{redbg}{\parbox{0.94\linewidth}{\small
\textbf{Careful --- a percent is not automatically a statistic.} The $61\%$ was computed from
the records of \blank{1.6cm} $900$ students --- every individual in the population. It
describes the \blank{1.8cm}, so it is a \blank{1.8cm}, even though it is a percent and even
though somebody had to compute it. The question is never \emph{what does the number look
like}; it is \emph{\blank{1.1cm} does it describe}.}}
\end{center}

\vspace{4pt}
Which kind do you almost always \emph{know}? The \blank{1.8cm}. \quad Which kind do you actually \emph{want}? The \blank{1.8cm}. You use the number you have to \textbf{estimate} the number you want --- that gap is the whole reason this course exists.

\end{notesbox}

\begin{notesbox}{2. Why We Sample at All --- and What Happens When We Do}
\small
If the manager wants a parameter, why not just ask all $800$? A \textbf{constraint} is a real-world limit on how a study can be run --- the reason a census is usually off the table.
\wordbank{time \;\textbullet\; cost \;\textbullet\; access \;\textbullet\; destruction \;\textbullet\; change \;\textbullet\; sampling variability \;\textbullet\; moves \;\textbullet\; stays put \;\textbullet\; do \;\textbullet\; closer \;\textbullet\; 1 \;\textbullet\; 3 \;\textbullet\; No \;\textbullet\; none of them}
\nopagebreak

\begin{multicols}{2}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.1em, nosep]
  \item \textbf{Time} --- reaching everyone takes longer than you have.
  \item \textbf{Cost} --- reaching everyone costs more than the budget.
  \item \textbf{Access} --- some individuals cannot be found, reached, or made to answer.
  \item \textbf{Destruction} --- measuring the individual ruins it.
  \item \textbf{Change} --- the population keeps changing while you collect.
\end{itemize}
\end{multicols}

\vspace{3pt}
Name the constraint that stands in the way in each situation.
\nopagebreak

\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.3}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{} X p{2.8cm} @{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Situation} & \textbf{Constraint} \\
\midrule
To check a cereal box's weight you must open it, and $12{,}000$ were filled Tuesday.
  & \blank{2.4cm} \\

  & \blank{2.4cm} \\

  & \blank{2.4cm} \\

\end{tabularx}

\vspace{6pt}
\textbf{\textcolor{cerulean}{The Statistic Moves. The Parameter Does Not.}}
Three volunteers each asked a \emph{different} $50$ shoppers on the \emph{same} Saturday
morning. Here is how many in each group said they drove.
\nopagebreak

\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.4}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{} l c X @{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Volunteer} & \textbf{Drove} & \textbf{Percent of that sample} \\
\midrule
Ana   & 26 & $26/50 = 0.52 = 52\%$ \\
Ben   & 24 & \blank{4.5cm} \\
Cleo  & 29 & \blank{4.5cm} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}

\vspace{3pt}
\begin{work}
  \text{Cleo: } \dfrac{29}{50} &= 0.58 = 58\%
\end{work}

\vspace{2pt}
How many different statistics did the three volunteers produce? \blank{1.4cm} \qquad
How many true percents are there for all $800$ Saturday shoppers? \blank{1.4cm}

\vspace{3pt}
Did one of the volunteers count wrong? \blank{1.2cm} \quad Which of the three found the true
percent for all $800$? \blank{3.2cm}

\vspace{5pt}
\begin{center}
\fcolorbox{cerulean}{frost}{\parbox{0.94\linewidth}{\small
\textbf{A different answer is not a wrong answer.} This change from one sample to the next
is called \blank{3.4cm}. It is not a mistake --- nobody miscounted. It is what samples
\blank{1cm}. The statistic \blank{1.4cm} from sample to sample; the parameter
\blank{2cm}, because the $800$ shoppers did not change. Each statistic is an \textbf{estimate} of the one parameter; a larger sample usually lands \blank{1.6cm} to it, but none lands exactly on it every time.}}
\end{center}

\end{notesbox}

\textbf{The Riverbend Job Survey --- we work this one together.} The Riverbend principal wants
to know what fraction of the school's $900$ students hold a part-time job. She asks $50$
students; $20$ of them have one.
\wordbank{Riverbend's 900 students \;\textbullet\; the 50 students she asked \;\textbullet\; statistic \;\textbullet\; estimate \;\textbullet\; parameter \;\textbullet\; 50 \;\textbullet\; 40 \;\textbullet\; 360 \;\textbullet\; 900 \;\textbullet\; No}
\textbf{(a)} Name the groups.

Population: \blank{5.4cm} \hfill Sample size: \blank{1.4cm}

Sample: \blank{5.4cm}

\vspace{3pt}
\textbf{(b)} What percent of the students she asked have a part-time job, and about how many of
Riverbend's $900$ students would that be?

\begin{work}
  \dfrac{20}{50}  &= 0.40 = 40\% \\
  0.40 \times 900 &= 360 \text{ students}
\end{work}

The $40\%$ is a \blank{1.8cm}. The $360$ is an \blank{1.8cm} of a \blank{1.8cm}.

\vspace{3pt}
\textbf{(c)} The same week, the assistant principal asks a \emph{different} $50$ students the
same question. In that group, $23$ have a part-time job.

\begin{work}
  \dfrac{23}{50} &= 0.46 = 46\%
\end{work}

One survey says $40\%$; the other says $46\%$. Did one of them make a mistake? Which survey
found the true percent for all $900$ students? Explain.
\par\writelines{2}

students.''}

\vspace{2pt}
