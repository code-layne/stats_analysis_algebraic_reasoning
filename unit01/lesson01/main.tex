\begin{tcolorbox}[colback=frost, colframe=cerulean, boxrule=0.9pt, arc=2mm,
    left=2mm, right=2mm, top=1.5mm, bottom=1.5mm]
    \textbf{Primary Objective:} Students will name the four stages of the data
    cycle and explain what each hands to the next, write a question that data can
    actually answer, decide from the question alone whether the data needed are
    categorical or quantitative, organize counts into a frequency table with
    percents, and write a conclusion that names the group it applies to ---
    including what the data do \emph{not} say.
\end{tcolorbox}

\boxguard
\begin{skillbox}[Priority Ideas \& Skills]{goldbox}
\begin{minipage}[t]{0.49\linewidth}\vspace{0pt}
    \textbf{Skills for this lesson}
    \begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.1em, itemsep=2pt, topsep=2pt]
      \item Name the four stages of the \textbf{data cycle} and state what each
            stage produces for the next. \hfill \textit{PS.DC.1a; AFDA.DA.2a}
      \item Test a question against three checks --- answers \textbf{vary}, the
            \textbf{group} is named, the \textbf{measurement} is named --- and
            repair one that fails. \hfill \textit{PS.DC.1b; AFDA.DA.2a}
      \item Decide from the \emph{question} whether the data required are
            categorical or quantitative. \hfill \textit{PS.DC.1c; AFDA.DA.2a}
      \item Organize collected values into a \textbf{frequency table} and convert
            counts to relative frequencies. \hfill \textit{PS.DC.1a}
      \item Write a \textbf{conclusion in context} and identify the stage at
            which a flawed study broke down. \hfill \textit{PS.DC.1a--b}
    \end{itemize}
\end{minipage}
\hfill
\begin{minipage}[t]{0.47\linewidth}\vspace{0pt}
    \textbf{Key Understandings}
    \begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.1em, itemsep=2pt, topsep=2pt]
      \item \emph{The stages are ordered because each one consumes the previous
            one's output.} A conclusion cannot name a group that stage 1 never
            named.
      \item The \textbf{question decides the data type}, not the other way
            around. You know before collecting anything whether you will end up
            with a mean or with percents.
      \item Stage 3 adds no information --- it makes information
            \emph{visible}. Sixty answers in a list and sixty answers in a
            frequency table are the same data; only one of them can be read.
      \item A conclusion may describe \textbf{only} the individuals you collected
            from, and may answer \textbf{only} the question you asked.
      \item It is a \textbf{cycle}: a good stage-4 answer produces the next
            stage-1 question. That is not failure --- that is how studies work.
    \end{itemize}
\end{minipage}
\end{skillbox}

\boxguard[24]
\begin{skillbox}[{Vocabulary, Concepts, \& Theorems}]{greenbox}
\small
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.35}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{} >{\bfseries}p{3.1cm} X @{}}
Data cycle & The four repeating stages of a study: \emph{formulate questions
       $\rightarrow$ collect or acquire data $\rightarrow$ organize and represent
       $\rightarrow$ analyze and communicate}. Also called the statistical
       cycle. \\
Statistical question & A question whose answers are expected to vary, that names
       the group it is about, and that says what will be measured. \\
Variable of interest & The characteristic the question says you will record for
       each individual. \\
Frequency & The count of individuals falling in one category or at one value. \\
Frequency table & A table pairing each category or value with its frequency. \\
Relative frequency & A count divided by the total, written as a decimal or a
       percent --- the share of the whole that group represents. \\
Conclusion in context & A sentence stating what the data show, naming the group
       it applies to and the units. \\
Population & The entire group the question is about. \\
Sample & The part of the population data were actually collected from.
       \emph{(Named formally in Lesson 1.2.)} \\
\end{tabularx}
\end{skillbox}

\boxguard
\begin{skillbox}[Hook]{frost}
    Put the council's problem on the board before anyone sits down:
    \begin{center}
      \textit{``We think a lot of students would bike to school if there were
      somewhere to park.''}
    \end{center}
    \textbf{Say:} the Student Council has one hour on the district's agenda, and
    the district does not act on ``we think.'' \textbf{Ask: what do they have to
    do first --- and in what order?} Take three or four answers and write them on
    the board in the order students give them, out of order if that is how they
    come. Then ask the class to \emph{sort} the board into a sequence. They will
    produce something close to the four stages themselves; name the stages over
    the top of their own list. Close by pointing at the last step: whatever they
    find, it has to end in a sentence someone else can check.
\end{skillbox}

\boxguard
\begin{skillbox}[Lesson]{frost}
\begin{multicols}{2}
\textbf{1. The four stages.} Fill in the table with them: formulate questions;
collect or acquire data; organize and represent; analyze and communicate.
\textbf{Do not just list them --- run the third column}, which says what each
stage \emph{hands to the next}. Ask: \emph{if stage 1 never says who the study is
about, what happens at stage 4?} Nothing can be said about anybody. That
dependency is the reason for the order.

\textbf{2. Stage 1 --- the three tests.} Answers vary; the group is named; the
measurement is named. Lesson 1.0 gave the first test only, so tests 2 and 3 are
the new material. Run the three weak questions and repair them aloud. ``How do I
get to school?'' fails test 1 (one person, one answer). ``Do people like
biking?'' fails test 2 --- and arguably test 3, since ``like'' is not something
you record.

\textbf{3. The question decides the type.} This is the lesson's pivot.
\textbf{Ask: do you have to collect the data to find out whether they are
categorical?} No --- ``how do students get to school'' can only produce labels.
Have students finish the sentence \emph{``for each student I will record
\underline{\hspace{1.5cm}}''} for each question in the table; if they cannot
finish it, the question is not ready.

\textbf{4. Stages 2 and 3.} The council asked 60 students. Build the frequency
table together, with the bus row done for them from the warm-up. Compute the bike
row in the work block, then check that the percents sum to $100\%$.
\textbf{Ask: what did stage 3 add?} Nothing --- the counts were already there. It
made them \emph{readable}. That is worth saying out loud, because students
routinely treat organizing as busywork.

\textbf{5. Stage 4.} Scale $10\%$ to $900$ students, then fill in the conclusion
sentence together. Now the item that matters: \textbf{does this survey show
students \emph{would} bike if there were racks?} No. The question asked how they
travel \emph{now}. Let that land before moving on.

\textbf{6. The cycle turns.} The unanswered question sends the council back to
stage 1. Then run the four broken scenarios as a whip-around: each one names a
stage. This is the format the exit ticket and homework both use.
\end{multicols}
\end{skillbox}

\boxguard
\begin{skillbox}[Explicit Instruction: Testing and Repairing a Question]{frost}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{} p{0.46\linewidth} X @{}}
\begin{minipage}[t]{\linewidth}\vspace{0pt}
\textbf{Steps --- give these verbatim}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.4em, itemsep=2pt, topsep=2pt]
  \item Read the question and ask: would two different individuals give
        \textbf{different answers}? If no, it fails test 1.
  \item Underline the \textbf{group}. If there is nothing to underline, it fails
        test 2.
  \item Finish this sentence: ``For each one I will record
        \underline{\hspace{1.6cm}}.'' If you cannot, it fails test 3.
  \item Repair it by supplying whatever was missing --- and change nothing else.
  \item Name the variable of interest, then classify it. Say how you will
        summarize it: a mean, or counts and percents.
\end{enumerate}
\end{minipage}
&
\begin{minipage}[t]{\linewidth}\vspace{0pt}
\textbf{Worked example --- ``Do people like biking?''}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.1em, itemsep=2pt, topsep=2pt]
  \item[1.] Different people would answer differently. \textbf{Passes test 1.}
  \item[2.] ``People'' --- which people? Everyone alive? \textbf{Fails test 2.}
  \item[3.] ``For each one I will record\dots'' their \emph{liking}? There is
        nothing to write down. \textbf{Fails test 3.}
  \item[4.] Repair: ``What percent of \textbf{Riverbend students} \textbf{bike to
        school}?''
  \item[5.] Variable: how the student travels. Categorical $\Rightarrow$ counts
        and percents.
\end{itemize}
\textit{Contrast: ``How many minutes is a Riverbend student's one-way trip?''
passes all three as written --- variable is trip minutes, quantitative,
summarized with a mean.}
\end{minipage}
\end{tabularx}
\end{skillbox}

\boxguard
\begin{skillbox}[Active Monitoring]{redbox}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em, itemsep=2pt, topsep=2pt]
  \item \textbf{Watch for stage 3 vs.\ stage 4 confusion.} The most common error
        today is calling any mistake ``stage 4,'' because that is where the wrong
        sentence appeared. Push back with: \emph{where did the problem enter?}
        A biased set of respondents entered at stage 2 even though it surfaced at
        stage 4.
  \item \textbf{Watch for repairs that change the topic.} Students often
        ``repair'' a question by writing a brand-new one. Require that the repair
        keeps the original subject and adds only what was missing.
  \item \textbf{Watch for percent-of-what.} In the frequency table, some students
        divide by the largest count instead of the total. Ask them to add their
        percents; if the sum is not $100\%$, they found their own error.
  \item \textbf{Cold-call prompts:} ``What does stage 2 hand to stage 3?''
        ``Name the variable of interest in that question.'' ``Which stage broke?''
        ``Say what this conclusion is \emph{not} allowed to claim.''
\end{itemize}
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Group Work \& Differentiation]{redbox}
\small
\begin{multicols}{3}
\textbf{Tier R --- Remediate}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1em, itemsep=1pt, topsep=2pt]
  \item Match four described steps of a finished study to their stages.
  \item Complete a frequency table with one row worked as a model.
  \item Name the variable of interest, classify it, and write one conclusion
        sentence.
\end{itemize}

\columnbreak
\textbf{Tier A --- Approaching Proficiency}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1em, itemsep=1pt, topsep=2pt]
  \item Choose the statistical question of two, repair the other, classify the
        variable.
  \item Percent from a sample, scaled to the population, plus a written
        conclusion.
  \item Diagnose two flawed reports by stage \emph{and} by what went wrong.
\end{itemize}

\columnbreak
\textbf{Tier E --- Extension}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1em, itemsep=1pt, topsep=2pt]
  \item Find \emph{two} separate overclaims in one printed sentence.
  \item Plan an original study across all four stages, question through
        conclusion.
  \item Explain how the same students yield categorical or quantitative data
        depending on the question asked.
\end{itemize}
\end{multicols}
\end{skillbox}

\boxguard
\begin{skillbox}[Individual Work \& Assessment]{redbox}
\textbf{Exit ticket (3 items, no notes).}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.4em, itemsep=2pt, topsep=2pt]
  \item Assign stage numbers to the four steps of a completed study (deliberately
        listed out of order).
  \item Name a test a weak question fails, repair it, then name the variable and
        its type.
  \item \textbf{Conceptual / justification check:} a study surveyed only the
        yearbook class and concluded about all Riverbend seniors. Which stage
        broke down, and why does that stop the conclusion? Correct answer:
        \emph{stage 2} --- the data describe only the class surveyed, so the
        conclusion may not be extended to seniors generally. Grade this one
        first.
\end{enumerate}
\textbf{Using the results:} sort into three piles --- (a) named stage 2 and said
why, (b) named stage 4 because that is where the bad sentence is, (c) restated
the sentence without diagnosing anything. Pile (b) is the teachable one and will
be large; open Lesson 1.2 by asking that group where the problem \emph{entered},
which is exactly the sample-vs.-population distinction 1.2 formalizes.
\end{skillbox}

\boxguard
\begin{skillbox}[Reinforcement \& Extension]{goldbox}
\textbf{Homework --- Lakeside Farmers Market.} A fresh context with the same
spine: name the variable of interest and its type, complete a frequency table
with relative frequencies, scale a sample percent to $800$ Saturday shoppers,
compute and \emph{interpret} a mean distance, judge three questions for
readiness, assign a stage to four failures, and close with a correct-arithmetic /
wrong-conclusion item that has to be answered in words.

\textbf{Extension (optional).} Students write a question they actually wonder
about and plan all four stages for it. Collect these --- the strongest ones are
reusable as starting points for the Lesson 1.8 class-survey project.

\textbf{Preview of Lesson 1.2.} The homework surveys $50$ shoppers and then talks
about all $800$. Next class names those two groups --- \emph{sample} and
\emph{population} --- and the two kinds of numbers computed from them,
\emph{statistic} and \emph{parameter}, plus the constraints that force us to
sample at all.

\textbf{Connections \& Big Ideas.} \textit{Unit 1 core idea:} a conclusion is only
as trustworthy as the study that produced it. \textit{Standards covered:}
PS.DC.1a, PS.DC.1b, PS.DC.1c; AFDA.DA.2a. \textit{Spirals forward to:} population
vs.\ sample and parameter vs.\ statistic (1.2, PS.DC.1d--e), sampling technique
(1.3, PS.DC.2a--b), bias (1.4, PS.DC.2c), and the full-cycle project (1.8).
\textit{Reaches back to:} Lesson 1.0's individuals, variables, and variable
types, and Algebra 1 percent and proportional reasoning.
\end{skillbox}

\begin{teachernote}[Warm-Up]
    Five minutes, silent, timed. Items 1 and 2 are the same numbers the guided
    notes open with, so this is a running start rather than a detour --- say so
    when you collect it. Item 3 is a Lesson 1.0 retrieval check: expect the
    classification to be right and the \emph{reason} column to be thin. Accept
    ``bus, car, walk, bike are labels'' but not ``because it's words''; the
    reason has to be that no arithmetic on those values means anything.
\end{teachernote}

\begin{teachernote}[Guided Notes]
    Pace: sections 1--2 in the first 15 minutes, section 3 in 8, sections 4--5 in
    12, section 6 in 5. \textbf{Section 3 is the lesson.} Lesson 1.0 classified
    variables sitting in a finished table; today students must decide the type
    from the \emph{question}, before any data exist. If you are short on time,
    compress section 4 (the arithmetic is warm-up-familiar) --- never section 3.

    Section 5's last question is the one students get wrong: the survey recorded
    how students travel \emph{now}, so it cannot speak to what they \emph{would}
    do. Do not resolve it quickly. It is the same reasoning the exit ticket
    item 3 and homework item 7 both test.

    In section 6, students will want to call every failure ``stage 4'' because
    that is where the bad sentence appears. Keep asking where the problem
    \emph{entered}.
\end{teachernote}

\begin{teachernote}[Group Activity]
    Groups of three, 15 minutes, tiers assigned rather than chosen. Tier R should
    finish early --- send those groups to Tier A item 1. Tier A item 3 is where
    the stage-2 versus stage-4 distinction gets tested; circulate there first.

    Tier E item 1 asks for \emph{two} overclaims in one sentence and students
    typically find one (the 40 cart customers are not all of Riverbend). The
    second is ``every morning'' --- they were asked about a single morning.
    Tier E item 2 doubles as an early draft for the Lesson 1.8 project; keep the
    good ones.
\end{teachernote}

\begin{teachernote}[Exit Ticket]
    Five minutes, notes closed, collected at the door. Item 1 should be near
    universal; if it is not, the stage names did not stick and Lesson 1.2 needs a
    two-minute reopen. Grade item 3 first and only item 3 if time is short.
    ``Stage 2'' with no reason is half credit --- the reason (the data describe
    only the yearbook class) is the part that transfers to 1.2's
    sample-versus-population work.
\end{teachernote}

\begin{teachernote}[Homework]
    Assign all seven items; about 25 minutes. Item 4(c) and item 7 cannot be
    answered by computing anything --- flag both when assigning, because students
    who treat this as a calculation sheet will skip exactly those two. Item 7 is
    the transfer item: the arithmetic is right and the conclusion is still wrong,
    and the missing piece (nobody was ever asked about a shuttle, and anyone kept
    away by the trip was never in the sample) is the bridge into Lesson 1.4's
    bias work. The optional extension is worth collecting for the 1.8 project.
\end{teachernote}
