\begin{tcolorbox}[colback=frost, colframe=cerulean, boxrule=0.9pt, arc=2mm,
    left=2mm, right=2mm, top=1.5mm, bottom=1.5mm]
    \textbf{Primary Objective:} Students will name the four stages of the data
    cycle and explain what each hands to the next, write a question that data can
    actually answer, decide from the question alone whether the data needed are
    categorical or quantitative, organize counts into a frequency table with
    percents, write a conclusion that names the group it applies to --- including
    what the data do \emph{not} say --- and name the stage at which a flawed study
    broke down.
    \\[2pt]
    \textbf{Standards:} PS.DC.1a, PS.DC.1b, PS.DC.1c; AFDA.DA.2a
\end{tcolorbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Priority Ideas \& Skills]{goldbox}
\begin{minipage}[t]{0.49\linewidth}\vspace{0pt}
    \textbf{Skills for this lesson}
    \begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.1em, itemsep=2pt, topsep=2pt]
      \item Name the four stages of the \textbf{data cycle} and state what each
            stage produces for the next. \hfill \textit{PS.DC.1a; AFDA.DA.2a}
      \item Test a question against three checks --- answers \textbf{vary}, the
            \textbf{group} is named, the \textbf{measurement} is named --- and
            repair one that fails. \hfill \textit{PS.DC.1b; AFDA.DA.2a}
      \item Decide from the \emph{question} whether the data required are
            categorical or quantitative. \hfill \textit{PS.DC.1c; AFDA.DA.2a}
      \item Organize collected values into a \textbf{frequency table} and convert
            counts to relative frequencies. \hfill \textit{PS.DC.1a}
      \item Write a \textbf{conclusion in context} and identify the stage at
            which a flawed study broke down. \hfill \textit{PS.DC.1a--b}
    \end{itemize}
\end{minipage}
\hfill
\begin{minipage}[t]{0.47\linewidth}\vspace{0pt}
    \textbf{Key Understandings}
    \begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.1em, itemsep=2pt, topsep=2pt]
      \item \emph{The stages are ordered because each one consumes the previous
            one's output.} A conclusion cannot name a group that stage 1 never
            named.
      \item The \textbf{question decides the data type}, not the other way
            around. You know before collecting anything whether you will end up
            with a mean or with percents.
      \item Stage 3 adds no information --- it makes information
            \emph{visible}. Sixty answers in a list and sixty answers in a
            frequency table are the same data; only one of them can be read.
      \item A conclusion may describe \textbf{only} the individuals you collected
            from, and may answer \textbf{only} the question you asked.
      \item \textbf{Target misconception:} students name the stage where the bad
            \emph{sentence} appeared (always stage 4) instead of the stage the
            problem \emph{entered} at. Ask ``where did it enter?'' every time.
    \end{itemize}
\end{minipage}
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[{Vocabulary, Concepts, \& Theorems}]{greenbox}
\small
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.35}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{} >{\bfseries}p{3.1cm} X @{}}
Data cycle & The four repeating stages of a study: \emph{formulate questions
       $\rightarrow$ collect or acquire data $\rightarrow$ organize and represent
       $\rightarrow$ analyze and communicate}. Also called the statistical
       cycle. \\
Statistical question & A question whose answers are expected to vary, that names
       the group it is about, and that says what will be measured. \\
Variable of interest & The characteristic the question says you will record for
       each individual. \\
Frequency & The count of individuals falling in one category or at one value. \\
Frequency table & A table pairing each category or value with its frequency. \\
Relative frequency & A count divided by the total, written as a decimal or a
       percent --- the share of the whole that group represents. \\
Conclusion in context & A sentence stating what the data show, naming the group
       it applies to and the units. \\
Population & The entire group the question is about. \\
Sample & The part of the population data were actually collected from.
       \emph{(Named formally in Lesson 1.2.)} \\
\end{tabularx}
\end{skillbox}

\boxguard
\begin{skillbox}[Hook]{frost}
    Put the council's problem on the board before anyone sits down:
    \begin{center}
      \textit{``We think a lot of students would bike to school if there were
      somewhere to park.''}
    \end{center}
    \textbf{Say:} the Student Council has one hour on the district's agenda, and
    the district does not act on ``we think.'' \textbf{Ask: what do they have to
    do first --- and in what order?} Take three or four answers and write them on
    the board in the order students give them, out of order if that is how they
    come. Then ask the class to \emph{sort} the board into a sequence. They will
    produce something close to the four stages themselves; name the stages over
    the top of their own list. Close by pointing at the last step: whatever they
    find, it has to end in a sentence someone else can check ---
    \textbf{and leave it there.} Whether the survey can support the council's
    ``would bike'' claim is not settled until section 5.
\end{skillbox}

\boxguard
\begin{skillbox}[Lesson --- Guided Notes (30 min)]{frost}
\begin{multicols}{2}
\textbf{1. The four stages (4 min).} Fill in the table with them: formulate
questions; collect or acquire data; organize and represent; analyze and
communicate. \textbf{Do not just list them --- run the third column}, which says
what each stage \emph{hands to the next}. Ask: \emph{if stage 1 never says who the
study is about, what happens at stage 4?} Nothing can be said about anybody. Then
model the second table: the council's own four actions, out of order, sorted into
stages ($2$, $4$, $1$, $3$). Do the first one aloud, then take the rest from the
class.

\textbf{2. Stage 1 --- the three tests (4 min).} Answers vary; the group is named;
the measurement is named. Lesson 1.0 gave the first test only, so tests 2 and 3
are the new material. Run the three weak questions and repair them aloud. ``How do
I get to school?'' fails test 1 (one person, one answer). ``Do people like
biking?'' fails test 2. ``Is the breakfast cart good?'' fails test 3 --- ``good''
is not something you write down. \textbf{Insist the repair keeps the original
subject}; that rule is tested again in the practice box.

\textbf{3. The question decides the type (6 min). \emph{This is the lesson.}}
\textbf{Ask: do you have to collect the data to find out whether they are
categorical?} No --- ``how do students get to school'' can only produce labels.
Have students finish the sentence \emph{``for each student I will record
\underline{\hspace{1.5cm}}''} for each question in the table; if they cannot
finish it, the question is not ready. Close on the last paragraph: \emph{same}
$30$ students, two questions, two types. The question decided it, not the people.

\textbf{4. Stages 2 and 3 (3 min).} The council asked 60 students. Build the
frequency table together, with the bus row done for them from the warm-up. Compute
the bike row in the work block, then check that the percents sum to $100\%$.
\textbf{Ask: what did stage 3 add?} Nothing --- the counts were already there. It
made them \emph{readable}. Say that out loud; students routinely treat organizing
as busywork.

\textbf{5. Stage 4 (3 min).} Scale $10\%$ to $900$ students, then fill in the
conclusion sentence together. Now the item that matters: \textbf{does this survey
show students \emph{would} bike if there were racks?} No. The question asked how
they travel \emph{now}. This is the hook, finally settled. Let it land.

\textbf{6. Where a study breaks down (4 min).} Four failed reports, one per stage.
\textbf{Give the rule first: ask where the problem \emph{entered}, not where the
bad sentence showed up.} Model row 1, then take rows 2--4 from the class and make
them defend the stage number. Row 2 (only bikers asked) is the one they will call
stage 4; row 4 (the sentence contradicts the counts) is the one that really is
stage 4. Close with the cycle-turns sentence and move.

\textbf{7. We Do --- the council's second survey (6 min). \emph{You hold the pen;
the class supplies every step.}} The cycle turned in section 6, so run it again:
$80$ students asked, $24$ would ride. Take the variable and its type from the
class, then the percent ($30\%$), then the scale-up ($270$), then the conclusion
sentence word by word --- make them supply the group, the number, and the
measurement separately. Finish with part (e): if only students already at the rack
had been asked, where would the problem have entered? \textbf{Stage 2.} This is
exactly the task the practice box releases, on the cart data.
\end{multicols}
\end{skillbox}

\boxguard
\begin{skillbox}[Explicit Instruction: Testing and Repairing a Question]{frost}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{} p{0.46\linewidth} X @{}}
\begin{minipage}[t]{\linewidth}\vspace{0pt}
\textbf{Steps --- give these verbatim}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.4em, itemsep=2pt, topsep=2pt]
  \item Read the question and ask: would two different individuals give
        \textbf{different answers}? If no, it fails test 1.
  \item Underline the \textbf{group}. If there is nothing to underline, it fails
        test 2.
  \item Finish this sentence: ``For each one I will record
        \underline{\hspace{1.6cm}}.'' If you cannot, it fails test 3.
  \item Repair it by supplying whatever was missing --- and change nothing else.
  \item Name the variable of interest, then classify it. Say how you will
        summarize it: a mean, or counts and percents.
\end{enumerate}
\end{minipage}
&
\begin{minipage}[t]{\linewidth}\vspace{0pt}
\textbf{Worked example --- ``Do people like biking?''}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.1em, itemsep=2pt, topsep=2pt]
  \item[1.] Different people would answer differently. \textbf{Passes test 1.}
  \item[2.] ``People'' --- which people? Everyone alive? \textbf{Fails test 2.}
  \item[3.] ``For each one I will record\dots'' their \emph{liking}? There is
        nothing to write down. \textbf{Fails test 3.}
  \item[4.] Repair: ``What percent of \textbf{Riverbend students} \textbf{bike to
        school}?''
  \item[5.] Variable: how the student travels. Categorical $\Rightarrow$ counts
        and percents.
\end{itemize}
\textit{Contrast: ``How many minutes is a Riverbend student's one-way trip?''
passes all three as written --- variable is trip minutes, quantitative,
summarized with a mean.}
\end{minipage}
\end{tabularx}
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Active Monitoring]{redbox}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em, itemsep=2pt, topsep=2pt]
  \item \textbf{Watch for stage 3 vs.\ stage 4 confusion} (notes section 6;
        practice item 3a). The most common error today is calling any mistake
        ``stage 4,'' because that is where the wrong sentence appeared. Push back
        with: \emph{where did the problem enter?} A biased set of respondents
        entered at stage 2 even though it surfaced at stage 4.
  \item \textbf{Watch for repairs that change the topic} (notes section 2).
        Students often ``repair'' a question by writing a brand-new one. Require
        that the repair keeps the original subject and adds only what was missing.
  \item \textbf{Watch for percent-of-what} (notes section 4; practice item 2).
        Some students divide by the largest count instead of the total. Ask them
        to add their percents; if the sum is not $100\%$, they found their own
        error.
  \item \textbf{Reject these two reasons all period:} ``because it's words'' for
        categorical, and ``stage 4 because that's where they said it.'' Require
        \emph{no arithmetic on those values means anything} and \emph{where it
        entered}, respectively.
  \item \textbf{Cold-call prompts:} ``What does stage 2 hand to stage 3?''
        ``Name the variable of interest in that question.'' ``Which stage broke?''
        ``Say what this conclusion is \emph{not} allowed to claim.''
\end{itemize}
\end{skillbox}

\boxguard
\begin{skillbox}[Independent Practice (10 min)]{redbox}
\small
\textbf{Silently, alone, no partners.} Students work the notes' closing practice
box, items 1--3, on the Riverbend breakfast cart. Say the rule out loud when you
release it --- this is the phase that drifts into partner work if you do not. The
gold \emph{Extension} item is for students who finish early, not a separate
assignment.

\vspace{2pt}
\textbf{The arc.} Item 1 is the on-ramp --- name the variable and its type, and
every student should be writing within thirty seconds, because it is section 3
with new nouns. Item 2 has its arithmetic \emph{pre-worked} in the work block, so
the ten minutes are spent on the conclusion sentence, which must name \emph{the
group she actually asked} --- the 40 cart customers, not the school.
\textbf{Item 3 is the crux}: the newspaper's sentence is arithmetically correct
and still wrong, and it is the second instance of the section-5 trap. Part (a) is
the stage (\textbf{2}); part (b) wants \emph{two} claims --- ``Riverbend
students'' when only cart customers were asked, and ``every morning'' when one
morning was recorded.

\vspace{2pt}
\textbf{Two-minute check.} By 2:00 every student should have item 1 answered and
be reading item 2. If more than a third do not, stop the room and re-read We Do
part (a) off the board.

\vspace{2pt}
\textbf{Circulate with questions, not answers.} \emph{Item 2, if the sentence is
bare:} ``Who does that number describe --- the 40, or the 900?'' \emph{Item 3, if
they wrote stage 4:} ``\textbf{Where did the problem \emph{enter}?} Who was never
asked?'' \emph{Item 3(b), if they found only one claim:} ``Read it again. How many
mornings did she watch?''

\vspace{2pt}
\textbf{Collect the work to display for the debrief} (ask permission): one item 2
conclusion that names the 40 and one that names the 900; \textbf{two disagreeing
item 3(a) answers} --- one stage 2, one stage 4; and one item 3(b) that found both
claims.
\end{skillbox}

\boxguard
\begin{skillbox}[Debrief (7 min) --- what goes on the board]{frost}
Run the displayed practice work in this order. \textbf{Students correct their own
answers in a second color} as you go; that self-correction is the record you
collect from.
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.3em, nosep, itemsep=3pt]
  \item \textbf{Item 1.} Variable and type, thirty seconds. This should be near
        universal --- if it is not, section 3 did not stick and Lesson 1.2 needs a
        two-minute reopen.
  \item \textbf{Item 2.} Put both displayed conclusions up. One names the 40
        students asked; one names all 900. Ask which one the data earned. The
        $315$ is a \emph{prediction under an assumption}, and the sentence has to
        say so.
  \item \textbf{Item 3 --- the crux.} Put the two disagreeing stage answers side
        by side and ask the question that separates them: \emph{where did the
        problem enter?} The biased set of respondents entered at \textbf{stage 2}
        even though the bad sentence appeared at stage 4. Then take both
        unsupported claims from part (b).
  \item \textbf{Extension}, if anyone reached it --- otherwise pose it aloud.
        A question that passes all three tests, its variable, and its type.
\end{enumerate}

\vspace{2pt}
\textbf{Check for understanding --- ask it cold, whole class.} \emph{``The
yearbook staff surveyed only the students in the yearbook class, then wrote:
Riverbend seniors prefer song lyrics. Which stage broke down, and why does that
stop the conclusion?''} Hands down, thirty seconds of think time, then cold-call
three students. Correct answer: \textbf{stage 2} --- the data describe only the
class surveyed, so the conclusion may not be extended to seniors generally.
\emph{This replaces the exit ticket.}

\vspace{2pt}
\textbf{Formative read.} Sort the answers into three piles as they come:
\textbf{(a)} named stage 2 and said why; \textbf{(b)} named stage 4 because that
is where the bad sentence is; \textbf{(c)} restated the sentence without
diagnosing anything. Pile (b) is the teachable one and will be large. Open Lesson
1.2 by asking that group where the problem \emph{entered} --- which is exactly the
sample-versus-population distinction 1.2 formalizes.
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Reinforcement \& Extension]{goldbox}
\textbf{Homework --- Lakeside Farmers Market.} A fresh context with the same
spine: name the variable of interest and its type, complete a frequency table
with relative frequencies, scale a sample percent to $800$ Saturday shoppers,
compute and \emph{interpret} a mean distance, judge three questions for
readiness, assign a stage to four failures, and close with a correct-arithmetic /
wrong-conclusion item that has to be answered in words.

\textbf{Scored} out of 20, due at the start of the next class; the cover's score column
carries a blank for it. Score items 1--5 for correctness and items 6--7 for the \emph{reason},
not the verdict.

\textbf{Packet or Desmos --- decide this lesson.} The packet set is authored either way.
Desmos Classroom carries percent-of-a-total and proportional-reasoning practice that would
cover items 2 and 3 adequately, but it has nothing for item 7's correct-arithmetic /
wrong-conclusion reasoning --- the item this lesson exists for --- so \textbf{assign the
packet pages for 1.1}. Say which one aloud at Close \& Assign so it is unambiguous.

\textbf{Extension (optional).} Students write a question they actually wonder
about and plan all four stages for it. Collect these --- the strongest ones are
reusable as starting points for the Lesson 1.8 class-survey project.

\textbf{Preview of Lesson 1.2.} The homework surveys $50$ shoppers and then talks
about all $800$. Next class names those two groups --- \emph{sample} and
\emph{population} --- and the two kinds of numbers computed from them,
\emph{statistic} and \emph{parameter}, plus the constraints that force us to
sample at all.

\textbf{Connections \& Big Ideas.} \textit{Unit 1 core idea:} a conclusion is only
as trustworthy as the study that produced it. \textit{Standards covered:}
PS.DC.1a, PS.DC.1b, PS.DC.1c; AFDA.DA.2a. \textit{Spirals forward to:} population
vs.\ sample and parameter vs.\ statistic (1.2, PS.DC.1d--e), sampling technique
(1.3, PS.DC.2a--b), bias (1.4, PS.DC.2c), and the full-cycle project (1.8).
\textit{Reaches back to:} Lesson 1.0's individuals, variables, and variable
types, and Algebra 1 percent and proportional reasoning.
\end{skillbox}

\begin{teachernote}[Warm-Up]
    Five minutes, silent, timed. Items 1 and 2 are the same numbers the guided
    notes open with, so this is a running start rather than a detour --- say so
    when you collect it. Item 3 is a Lesson 1.0 retrieval check: expect the
    classification to be right and the \emph{reason} column to be thin. Accept
    ``bus, car, walk, bike are labels'' but not ``because it's words''; the
    reason has to be that no arithmetic on those values means anything.
\end{teachernote}

\begin{teachernote}[Guided Notes]
    Pace: $4 + 4 + 6 + 3 + 3 + 4 + 6 = 30$. \textbf{Section 3 is the lesson.}
    Lesson 1.0 classified variables sitting in a finished table; today students
    must decide the type from the \emph{question}, before any data exist. If you
    are short on time, compress section 4 (the arithmetic is warm-up-familiar) or
    take rows 3--4 of section 6 aloud without writing --- \textbf{never} section
    3, and never the We Do.

    The single highest-leverage move is refusing to settle the hook early.
    Section 5's last question --- does the survey show students \emph{would} bike?
    --- is the one students get wrong, and it only lands because they committed to
    an answer at the door and carried it through four sections. Do not resolve it
    in section 1 when someone guesses it.

    Section 6 is where the four broken studies live, one per stage. Give the rule
    (\emph{where did the problem enter?}) \emph{before} the table, not after, or
    students will write ``4'' four times. Row 4 is the only true stage-4 failure
    and it is there so ``stage 4'' means something rather than being the default.

    \textbf{Why the We Do runs the cart's task on the council's data:} section 7
    is the same five moves the practice box releases --- variable and type,
    percent, scale-up, conclusion, diagnose --- worked jointly on the council's
    second survey. Students hold a completed template before they work the cart
    alone. Do not summarize it; work every step from the class's answers.
    Pre-working item 2's arithmetic in the practice box is what buys the ten
    minutes for the conclusion sentence and item 3.
\end{teachernote}

\begin{teachernote}[Homework]
    Assign all seven items; about 25 minutes. Item 4(c) and item 7 cannot be
    answered by computing anything --- flag both when assigning, because students
    who treat this as a calculation sheet will skip exactly those two. Grade item
    7 first: it is the transfer item, the arithmetic is right and the conclusion
    is still wrong, and the missing piece (nobody was ever asked about a shuttle,
    and anyone kept away by the trip was never in the sample) is the bridge into
    Lesson 1.4's bias work. Desmos does not carry that reasoning, which is why
    1.1 is a packet night. Go over item 7 at the start of Lesson 1.2. The optional
    extension is worth collecting for the 1.8 project.
\end{teachernote}
