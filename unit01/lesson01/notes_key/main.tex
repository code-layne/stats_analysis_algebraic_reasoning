\begin{objectivebox}
\small
By the end of this lesson, I will be able to\dots
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.5em, itemsep=2pt, topsep=2pt]
  \item name the four stages of the \textbf{data cycle} and say what each stage
        produces for the next one;
  \item write a question that data can actually answer --- one whose answers
        \emph{vary}, that names the \emph{group}, and that says what will be
        \emph{measured};
  \item decide from the question alone whether the data I need are
        \textbf{categorical} or \textbf{quantitative};
  \item organize collected data into a frequency table and turn counts into
        percents;
  \item write a conclusion that names the group it applies to --- and say what
        the data do \emph{not} tell me;
  \item name the stage at which a flawed study \emph{broke down}.
\end{enumerate}
\end{objectivebox}

\boxguard
\begin{vocabbox}
\small
Fill in each term as we build it together.
\vocabans{Data cycle}{The four repeating stages of a study: formulate questions;
collect or acquire data; organize and represent data; analyze data and
communicate results.}
\vocabans{Statistical question}{A question whose answers are expected to vary,
that names the group it is about, and that says what will be measured.}
\vocabans{Variable of interest}{The characteristic the question says you will
record for each individual.}
\vocabans{Frequency table}{A table listing each category or value together with
how many individuals fall in it.}
\vocabans{Relative frequency}{A count divided by the total, written as a decimal
or a percent --- what share of the whole that group is.}
\vocabans{Conclusion in context}{A sentence stating what the data show, naming
the group it applies to and the units.}
\end{vocabbox}

\boxguard[12]
\begin{hookbox}
\small
The Riverbend Student Council has one hour on the district's agenda. They want to
ask for \textbf{more bike racks} outside the main entrance.

\begin{center}
\textit{``We think a lot of students would bike if there were somewhere to park.''}
\end{center}

The district will not act on ``we think.'' \textbf{What would the council have to
do first --- and in what order?} Write the first two steps you would take.
\par\ansline{Answers vary. First decide exactly what to ask and about whom; then collect data.}
\ansline{Also accept: ask students how they travel now, then count the answers by group.}
\end{hookbox}

\boxguard[20]
\begin{notesbox}{1. The Four Stages of the Data Cycle}
\small
Every study in this course runs the same four stages, in order --- and then
starts over. Fill in the name of each stage as we go.
\nopagebreak

\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.35}
\begin{tabularx}{0.97\linewidth}{@{} c p{4.3cm} X @{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Stage} & \textbf{Name} & \textbf{What it hands to the next stage} \\
\midrule
1 & \ans{Formulate questions} & A question that names a group and says what will be measured. \\
2 & \ans{Collect or acquire data} & The raw values --- one row per individual. \\
3 & \ans{Organize and represent} & A table or display that makes the pattern visible. \\
4 & \ans{Analyze and communicate} & A conclusion, stated in context, plus the next question. \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}

\vspace{3pt}
\textbf{Why the order matters:} each stage can only use what the stage before it
handed over. If stage 1 never says \emph{who} the study is about, then stage 4
cannot say who the conclusion applies to.

\vspace{4pt}
Here is what the council actually did last spring, out of order. Write the stage
number beside each one.
\nopagebreak

\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.35}
\begin{tabularx}{0.95\linewidth}{@{} X c @{}}
\toprule
\textbf{What the Student Council did} & \textbf{Stage} \\
\midrule
Asked $60$ students how they usually get to school.  & \ans{2} \\
Told the district what the survey showed.            & \ans{4} \\
Decided to ask how Riverbend students travel.        & \ans{1} \\
Counted the answers into a table by travel method.   & \ans{3} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}
\end{notesbox}

\boxguard
\begin{notesbox}{2. Stage 1 --- A Question Data Can Answer}
\small
A \textbf{statistical question} has to pass three tests.
\nopagebreak

\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.3}
\begin{tabularx}{0.95\linewidth}{@{} c X @{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Test} & \textbf{Ask yourself} \\
\midrule
1 & Do the answers \ans{vary} from one individual to the next? \\
2 & Does the question name the \ans{group} it is about? \\
3 & Does it say what will be \ans{measured} or recorded? \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}

\vspace{4pt}
Each question below fails at least one test. Say which test it fails, then repair
it --- \textbf{keep the original subject and put back only what was missing.}
\nopagebreak

\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.5}
\begin{tabularx}{0.97\linewidth}{@{} p{4.4cm} p{1.5cm} X @{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Weak question} & \textbf{Fails} & \textbf{Repaired question} \\
\midrule
How do I get to school?          & \ans{1} & \ans{How do Riverbend students get to school?} \\
Do people like biking?           & \ans{2} & \ans{What percent of Riverbend students bike to school?} \\
Is the breakfast cart good?      & \ans{3} & \ans{How many mornings a week do Riverbend students buy breakfast?} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}

\vspace{2pt}
\textit{The council's real question: ``How do Riverbend students usually get to
school?'' Answers vary, the group is named, and what gets recorded is clear.}
\end{notesbox}

\boxguard
\begin{notesbox}{3. The Question Decides the Type of Data}
\small
You do not wait until the data arrive to find out what type they are. The
\textbf{question} tells you --- and the type tells you how you will be allowed to
summarize the answers.
\nopagebreak

\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.5}
\begin{tabularx}{0.98\linewidth}{@{} X p{3.1cm} p{2.2cm} p{2.4cm} @{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Question} & \textbf{What you record} & \textbf{Type} &
\textbf{Summarize with} \\
\midrule
How do Riverbend students usually get to school?
  & \ans{way of traveling} & \ans{categorical} & \ans{percents} \\
How many minutes is a Riverbend student's one-way trip?
  & \ans{trip minutes} & \ans{quantitative} & \ans{a mean} \\
How old are the bikes students ride to school?
  & \ans{bike age in years} & \ans{quantitative} & \ans{a mean} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}

\vspace{3pt}
The thing you record is the \textbf{variable of interest}. Naming it out loud is
the fastest way to catch a question that is not ready:
if you cannot finish the sentence \emph{``for each student I will
record \underline{\hspace{2.6cm}},''} the question is not finished either.

\vspace{3pt}
\textbf{Same students, different question, different type.} Ask $30$ students
\emph{how many mornings a week} they eat breakfast at school and you record a
count --- \ans{quantitative}. Ask the same $30$ students whether they \emph{ever} eat
breakfast at school and you record yes or no --- \ans{categorical}. The
\emph{question} decided the type, not the students.
\end{notesbox}

\boxguard[30]
\begin{notesbox}{4. Stages 2 and 3 --- Collect, Then Organize}
\small
The council asked $60$ Riverbend students how they usually get to school. That is
stage 2. Now organize the $60$ answers into a \textbf{frequency table} --- counts
first, then each count as a percent of the total.
\nopagebreak

\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.4}
\begin{tabularx}{0.9\linewidth}{@{} l c X @{}}
\toprule
\textbf{How they get to school} & \textbf{Frequency (count)} &
\textbf{Relative frequency (percent)} \\
\midrule
Bus   & 27 & $27/60 = 0.45 = 45\%$ \\
Car   & 15 & \ans{$15/60 = 0.25 = 25\%$} \\
Walk  & 12 & \ans{$12/60 = 0.20 = 20\%$} \\
Bike  &  6 & \ans{$6/60 \; = 0.10 = 10\%$} \\
\midrule
\textbf{Total} & \ans{60} & \ans{$60/60 = 1.00 = 100\%$} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}

\vspace{3pt}
Show the work for the \textbf{Bike} row:

\begin{work}
  \dfrac{6}{60} &= 0.10 \\
                &= 10\%
\end{work}

\vspace{2pt}
\textbf{Check:} the four percents must add to \ans{100\%}. If they do not, a
count is wrong or a student was left out.

\vspace{2pt}
\textit{Notice what stage 3 did: the counts were already there at stage 2, but
until they were organized, nobody could see that biking is the smallest group.}
\end{notesbox}

\boxguard
\begin{notesbox}{5. Stage 4 --- Analyze and Communicate}
\small
A conclusion is not a number. It is a sentence that says \textbf{what the data
show}, \textbf{who it applies to}, and \textbf{in what units}.

\vspace{3pt}
Scale the sample percent up to the whole school ($900$ students):

\begin{work}
  0.10 \times 900 &= 90 \text{ students}
\end{work}

\vspace{3pt}
Fill in the council's conclusion:

\vspace{2pt}
\textit{``Of the \ans{60} students we surveyed, \ans{10}\% bike to
school. If our sample is representative, that is about \ans{90} of
Riverbend's \ans{900} students.''}

\vspace{4pt}
\textbf{Now the hard part --- what the data do \emph{not} say.} The council wants
to claim students \emph{would} bike if there were racks. Does this survey support
that claim? \ans{No}

Why not? \ansline{The question asked how students travel now, not what they would do if racks existed.}
\end{notesbox}

\boxguard[26]
\begin{notesbox}{6. Where a Study Breaks Down}
\small
When a study goes wrong, ask \textbf{where the problem entered} --- not where the
bad sentence showed up. Each report below failed at a \emph{different} stage.
\nopagebreak

\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.5}
\begin{tabularx}{0.97\linewidth}{@{} X c p{4.2cm} @{}}
\toprule
\textbf{What went into the council's report} & \textbf{Stage} &
\textbf{What went wrong} \\
\midrule
They set out to ask ``Is biking good?'' and could not decide what to write down
for each student.
  & \ans{1} & \ans{never said what to record} \\
Only students locking bikes at the rack were asked, and the report says
``Riverbend students bike to school.''
  & \ans{2} & \ans{only bikers were asked} \\
All $60$ answers were written out as a paragraph and never counted by group.
  & \ans{3} & \ans{never counted by group} \\
The table shows $6$ of $60$ bike, and the report says ``most Riverbend students
bike.''
  & \ans{4} & \ans{the sentence ignores the counts} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}

\vspace{3pt}
\textbf{And the cycle turns.} The council's answer produced a \emph{new} question
--- ``How many students \emph{would} bike if there were racks?'' --- and no data
on hand can answer it. So they go back to stage \ans{1} and run the cycle
again.
\end{notesbox}

\boxguard[24]
\begin{notesbox}{7. We Do --- The Council's Second Survey}
\small
The council runs the cycle again. New question: \emph{``How many Riverbend
students would ride a bike to school if there were racks?''} They ask $80$
students, and $24$ say they would. \textbf{We will run all four stages together.}

\vspace{3pt}
\textbf{(a)} What is the variable of interest, and what type is it?

Variable: \ans{whether the student would ride if there were racks} \hfill Type: \ans{categorical}

\vspace{3pt}
\textbf{(b)} What percent of the students asked said they would?

\begin{work}
  \dfrac{24}{80} &= 0.30 \\
                 &= 30\%
\end{work}

\textbf{(c)} Riverbend has $900$ students. Predict how many would ride.

\begin{work}
  0.30 \times 900 &= 270 \text{ students}
\end{work}

\textbf{(d)} Write the conclusion the council can hand to the district. Name the
group, the number, and what was measured.
\par\ansline{Of the 80 Riverbend students we asked, 30\% said they would ride a bike to school if}
\ansline{there were racks --- about 270 of Riverbend's 900 students.}

\vspace{2pt}
\textbf{(e)} Suppose the council had asked only the $80$ students already
standing at the bike rack. Which stage would the problem have entered at, and
what could the report no longer claim?
\par\ansline{Stage 2 --- only students who already bike were asked, so it cannot speak for the school.}
\end{notesbox}

\boxguard[20]
\begin{practicebox}
\small
\textbf{Work these on your own.} The Riverbend cafeteria manager put a
\textbf{breakfast cart} in the main hallway for a week. On Friday she asked the
$40$ students leaving the cart, \emph{``What did you buy at the cart this
morning?''} Her answers: bagel $14$, fruit cup $10$, muffin $12$, nothing $4$.

\vspace{3pt}
\textbf{1.} What was the variable of interest --- what did she record for each
student? What type is it?

Variable: \ans{what the student bought at the cart} \hfill Type: \ans{categorical}

\vspace{3pt}
\textbf{2.} What percent of the students she asked bought a bagel? And if cart
customers were typical of the school, about how many of Riverbend's $900$
students would that be?

\begin{work}
  \dfrac{14}{40}  &= 0.35 = 35\% \\
  0.35 \times 900 &= 315 \text{ students}
\end{work}

Write the conclusion as one sentence, naming \emph{the group she actually asked},
the number, and what was measured.
\par\ansline{Of the 40 students leaving the breakfast cart on Friday, 35\% bought a bagel. If cart}
\ansline{customers are typical of the school, that predicts about 315 of the 900 students.}

\vspace{3pt}
\textbf{3.} The school newspaper prints: \emph{``$35\%$ of Riverbend students buy
a bagel every morning.''} The arithmetic behind the $35\%$ is correct.

\textbf{(a)} That sentence describes \emph{all} Riverbend students. Which stage
did the problem enter at? \ans{Stage 2}

\textbf{(b)} Name \emph{two} separate things the sentence claims that Friday's
data cannot support.
\par\ansline{(1) Only the 40 students leaving the cart were asked, not all Riverbend students ---}
\ansline{students who never go near the cart were never in the data.}
\ansline{(2) They were asked about one morning, so ``every morning'' was never measured.}
\end{practicebox}
