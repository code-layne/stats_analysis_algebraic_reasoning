    \textbf{Primary Objective:} Students will compare a controlled experiment
    with an observational study point by point, state and \emph{justify} the
    strongest conclusion each design earns, decide when an experiment is
    unethical, impossible, or impractical to run and say what to run instead,
    plan a well-designed experiment through the four stages of the statistical
    cycle, and select a data collection method --- survey, interview, focus
    group, observation, or content analysis --- that fits a given context.
\end{tcolorbox}

    \begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.1em, itemsep=2pt, topsep=2pt]

      \item Decide whether a question can be answered by an experiment at
            all --- \textbf{ethical, possible, practical}.
            \hfill \textit{PS.DC.3c}
      \item Plan a well-designed experiment through the four stages of the
            \textbf{statistical cycle}. \hfill \textit{PS.DC.3d}
      \item Select a \textbf{data collection method} --- survey, interview,
            focus group, observation, content analysis --- for a context.
            \hfill \textit{PS.DC.3e}
      \item Separate the \emph{method} (observation) from the \emph{design}
            (observational study). \hfill \textit{PS.DC.3c, 3e}
    \end{itemize}
\end{minipage}
\hfill
\begin{minipage}[t]{0.47\linewidth}\vspace{0pt}
    \textbf{Key Understandings}
    \begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.1em, itemsep=2pt, topsep=2pt]
      \item \emph{In an observational study we measure or survey without
            trying to affect anyone; in a controlled experiment we assign and
            apply a treatment.} One row of the comparison table produces every
            other row.
      \item An experiment is the stronger design, so the real decision is not
            ``which is better'' but \textbf{which one can I run}.
      \item You cannot assign what a person already \emph{is} or \emph{has}.
            That single test settles most of the sorting.
      \item ``We could not run an experiment'' is a reason for a
            \emph{weaker} verb, not an excuse for a stronger one.
      \item The cycle does not change from 1.5 to 1.7. What an experiment adds
            lives inside stage two, \emph{Collect}.
      \item Study type and collection method are independent choices ---
            an experiment can collect its data by observation.
    \end{itemize}
\end{minipage}
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[{Vocabulary, Concepts, \& Theorems}]{greenbox}
\small
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.35}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{} >{\bfseries}p{3.1cm} X @{}}
Controlled experiment & A study in which the researcher assigns the units to
       groups by chance and imposes a treatment on at least one group. It can
       support a cause-and-effect conclusion. \\
Observational study & A study in which the researcher measures or surveys
       individuals without assigning anyone to a group. It can report an
       association only. \\
Statistical cycle & Formulate $\rightarrow$ collect $\rightarrow$ represent
       $\rightarrow$ analyze \& report. The same four stages plan either study
       type. \\
Survey & The same fixed questions given to every individual in a chosen sample.
       Best for counts, percents, and attitudes across many people. \\
Interview & A one-on-one conversation built on planned questions. Best for
       depth --- the reasons behind an answer, in the person's own words. \\
Focus group & A small-group discussion led by a facilitator, where members
       build on each other's answers. \\
Observation & Recording what people actually do, using the senses. Best when
       behavior and self-report may disagree. \\
Content analysis & Studying records or documents that already exist ---
       borrowing histories, transcripts, past scores. Best for change over
       time. \\
\end{tabularx}
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Hook]{frost}
    Open by declaring the Study Center question \emph{closed} --- last lesson
    settled it, and students should feel the win. Then change the question:
    \textbf{do students who ride the bus more than $45$ minutes each way score
    lower on the state test?} Ask for the design they just learned. Somebody
    will start describing an experiment; let them get two sentences in before
    you ask the one question that stops it: \emph{what would you have to assign
    a student?} A $45$-minute bus ride --- which means assigning where their
    family lives. Nobody can do that. \textbf{So the strongest design is off the
    table, and the question is still a real question.} That gap is the lesson:
    when you cannot assign, you do not give up and you do not overclaim --- you
    run the best observational study you can and you write a weaker sentence.
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Lesson]{frost}
\begin{multicols}{2}
\textbf{1. Side by side.} The four-row comparison table is the spine of
PS.DC.3c and the first two rows are given, so this moves fast. \textbf{Ask on
each blank: what does the researcher \emph{do} here?} The payoff is the second
table --- the two Riverbend studies with the sentence each one earns. Insist on
the exact verb: \emph{is associated with} in row one, \emph{caused} in row two.
A student who writes ``the Study Center is bad'' in row one has not written a
statistical sentence.

\textbf{2. When you cannot assign.} Three reasons, then a four-row sorting
table. \textbf{The test to give them out loud: can you hand this out to a
person?} Smoking, retirement, and a long bus ride all fail it; a reading block
passes. The closing pair of blanks --- \emph{association}, never a
\emph{cause-and-effect claim} --- is the sentence to hold them to for the rest
of the unit.

\textbf{3. The cycle, for an experiment.} Deliberately the same table shape as
Lesson 1.5's, so put the two side by side if you still have 1.5's notes. Only
\emph{Collect} changes, and it changes by one word. \textbf{Ask what 1.5's
Collect row said and what this one says} --- the answer is \emph{sampled}
against \emph{assigned}.

\textbf{4. Five methods.} The reference table is given; the work is the
five-row matching table. Do the first row together and let them finish. Close
on the trap sentence at the bottom: Bayside's experiment collected data by
\emph{observation} and was still an \emph{experiment}. Say plainly that these
are two different questions with two different answers.

\textbf{Guided practice.} Cedar Ridge, three questions, three rows. Question one
can be assigned, questions two and three cannot. The closing write is the exit
ticket in miniature and asks for the honest sentence plus one competing
variable --- the new bins, or newer tenants.
\end{multicols}
\end{skillbox}

\boxguard
\begin{skillbox}[Explicit Instruction: Choosing the Study and the Method]{frost}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{} p{0.46\linewidth} X @{}}
\begin{minipage}[t]{\linewidth}\vspace{0pt}
\textbf{Steps --- give these verbatim}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.4em, itemsep=2pt, topsep=2pt]
  \item \textbf{Write the question.} What do you want to know, and about whom?
  \item \textbf{Can you assign the treatment?} Could you hand it to a person
        by chance?
  \item If \textbf{no}, say which reason: unethical, impossible, or
        impractical. Then it is an \textbf{observational study}.
  \item If \textbf{yes}, it is an \textbf{experiment} --- plan comparison,
        randomization, replication, control.
  \item \textbf{Walk the cycle:} formulate, collect (this is where the
        assigning or the sampling lives), represent, analyze \& report.
  \item \textbf{Choose the method} from what the question needs: many people
        $\Rightarrow$ survey; \emph{why} $\Rightarrow$ interview; a group
        building on itself $\Rightarrow$ focus group; what people \emph{do}
        $\Rightarrow$ observation; records already written $\Rightarrow$
        content analysis.
  \item \textbf{Write the conclusion before you see the data.} Experiment
        $\Rightarrow$ a causal verb is allowed. Observational $\Rightarrow$
        \emph{is associated with}, and nothing stronger.
\end{enumerate}
\end{minipage}
&
\begin{minipage}[t]{\linewidth}\vspace{0pt}
\textbf{Worked example --- ``Harbor Point wants to know whether a weekly
water-aerobics class reduces reported joint pain among its $1{,}500$
members.''}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.1em, itemsep=2pt, topsep=2pt]
  \item[1.] Question: does the class reduce reported joint pain, for Harbor
        Point members?
  \item[2.] Can she assign a water-aerobics class? Yes --- she runs the class
        and picks who is in it.
  \item[4.] Experiment. $120$ volunteers, a generator sends $60$ to the class
        and $60$ to none; six weeks; nothing else changed.
  \item[5.] Collect: assign, then run. Represent: a two-row table of counts and
        percents. Analyze: $42/60 = 70\%$ against $30/60 = 50\%$, a $20$-point
        gap; pooled $72/120 = 60\%$, about $900$ of $1{,}500$ members.
  \item[6.] Method: \emph{observation} of the class rosters plus a short
        \emph{survey} of reported pain --- the method is not the design.
  \item[7.] ``The weekly class caused less reported joint pain.'' Allowed:
        chance built the groups.
\end{itemize}
\textit{Now change one word --- ``members who \emph{already have} arthritis''
--- and step 2 fails. Same director, same pool, an observational study and a
weaker sentence.}
\end{minipage}
\end{tabularx}
\end{skillbox}

\boxguard
\begin{skillbox}[Active Monitoring]{redbox}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em, itemsep=2pt, topsep=2pt]
  \item \textbf{Watch for ``experiments are better, so use an experiment.''}
        True and useless. The question is whether one can be run at all ---
        push back with \emph{what would you assign?}
  \item \textbf{Watch for ``it would be unethical'' used for everything.} Only
        one of the three reasons is about harm. Retirement, arthritis, and a
        bus ride are \emph{impossible}, not unethical.
  \item \textbf{Watch for the observation / observational-study collision.}
        It is the single likeliest error in this lesson. Ask the two questions
        separately: \emph{who built the groups?} then \emph{how did the data
        get recorded?}
  \item \textbf{Watch for a method chosen by convenience.} ``A survey, because
        it is easy'' is not a justification. Make them name what the question
        needs --- a count, a reason, a behavior, or a record.
  \item \textbf{Watch for a causal verb smuggled into an observational
        conclusion.} \emph{Leads to, results in, boosts} are all the banned
        verb wearing a hat.
  \item \textbf{Cold-call prompts:} ``What would you have to assign?'' ``Which
        of the three reasons?'' ``What sentence does this study earn?'' ``What
        does the question need --- a count or a reason?'' ``Method or design?''
\end{itemize}
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Individual Work \& Assessment]{redbox}
\textbf{Exit ticket (3 items, no notes).} One context: Bayside Middle School,
two studies already on file plus one new question.
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.4em, itemsep=2pt, topsep=2pt]
  \item Compute $90 \div 150 = 60\%$, then label Study A observational and
        Study B an experiment, and say what earns Study B the word
        \emph{caused}.
  \item The long-bus-ride question: she cannot assign where a family lives, so
        it must be observational. A student who writes ``unethical'' has the
        right verdict from the wrong reason --- half credit.
  \item \textbf{Conceptual / justification check:} choosing a method for
        \emph{why} sixth-graders skip breakfast. Full credit names interviews
        (or a focus group) \emph{and} says what a fixed survey cannot do ---
        follow up, or ask what an answer means. Grade this one first.
\end{enumerate}
\textbf{Using the results:} sort into three piles --- (a) matched the method to
what the question needs, (b) picked a defensible method with no reason attached,
(c) picked a survey because everything is a survey. Pile (c) walks into Lesson
1.8 planning to hand out a questionnaire no matter what their class question
turns out to be; seat those students together on day one of the project and make
them justify the method before they write a single item.
\end{skillbox}

\begin{skillbox}[Reinforcement \& Extension]{goldbox}
\textbf{Homework --- Millbrook Public Library, the third study.} The library
that was watched in 1.5 and assigned in 1.6 now runs a book-club experiment:
$300$ volunteers, a generator sends $150$ to a weekly evening club and $150$ to
none. $96$ and $60$ read at least $10$ books. Students sort the two earlier
studies by type, compute $64\%$ and $40\%$ and the $24$-point gap, pool the
volunteers ($156/300 = 52\%$, scaled to $624$ of $1{,}200$), fill the four
stages of the cycle for the new study, decide for three fresh questions whether
assignment is possible, match five jobs to the five collection methods, explain
why the largest of the library's three gaps ($48$ points) is its
\emph{weakest} evidence, and rewrite a newspaper headline that claims an
observational study \emph{proves} a cause.

\textbf{Extension (optional).} Students finish planning the study they proposed
for Lesson 1.6's extension: whether it can be run as an experiment at all, the
four stages in one line each, the collection method with a reason, and the
conclusion sentence written \emph{before} the data. With the 1.2--1.6
extensions this is a complete study proposal --- which is exactly what Lesson
1.8's project opens with.

\textbf{Preview of Lesson 1.8.} The class runs its own study: choose a question,
plan the cycle, pick a sampling technique and a collection method, collect real
data from classmates, and report a conclusion the design earns
(AFDA.DA.2d, h--j; PS.DC.2d).

\textbf{Connections \& Big Ideas.} \textit{Unit 1 core idea:} a conclusion is
only as trustworthy as the study that produced it. \textit{Standards covered:}
PS.DC.3c, PS.DC.3d, PS.DC.3e --- with PS.DC.1b carried forward in every
conclusion item. \textit{Spirals forward to:} the class study project (1.8),
comparing two distributions (2.7), two-way tables and association (3.1),
regression and the correlation-is-not-causation warning (5.4--5.6), and
inference, where the design decided here is what a $p$-value is finally allowed
to speak about (7.5--7.6, 8.4--8.5). \textit{Reaches back to:} Lesson 1.1's
statistical cycle, Lesson 1.3's sampling techniques, Lesson 1.4's bias, Lesson
1.5's observational study, and Lesson 1.6's four principles.
\end{skillbox}

\begin{teachernote}[Warm-Up]
    Five minutes, silent, timed. Nothing here is new --- items 1 and 2 are the
    same two divisions the class has run since 1.2, and item 3(a) is a
    subtraction.

    The point of the warm-up is item 3(b), which is not arithmetic at all. The
    two studies' group sizes are $60/90$ and $60/60$, and students should be
    able to say \emph{why}: in the first study the students sorted themselves,
    so the sizes came out however they came out; in the second a generator split
    an even number of volunteers. Unequal group sizes are a symptom of
    self-selection. Say that out loud at the collection --- it is the first
    row of the notes' comparison table, arriving thirty seconds early.

    Leave all six numbers on the board: $65\%$, $80\%$, $15$ points, $75\%$,
    $55\%$, $20$ points. Both of the notes' first two tables run on them, and
    the hook needs them immediately.
\end{teachernote}

\begin{teachernote}[Guided Notes]
    Pace: section 1 in 12 minutes, section 2 in 12, section 3 in 8, section 4 in
    12, guided practice in 8. \textbf{Sections 1 and 4 carry the standards that
    will be assessed}; section 3 is a mirror of a table students filled in
    Lesson 1.5 and should move quickly.

    Section 1's first two rows are given on purpose. The remaining blanks are
    short --- ``chance,'' ``yes,'' ``not necessarily,'' ``an association only''
    --- so the time goes into the \emph{second} table, where students write the
    two Riverbend sentences. Do not accept an evaluation (``the Study Center
    is bad'') in place of a statistical sentence. Model the wording once and
    require it after that.

    Section 2 is where the lesson earns its place after 1.6. The sorting table's
    test is \emph{can you hand this out to a person?} Run row two aloud --- you
    cannot assign a person to smoke, and even if you could, you would not.
    Rows three and four are \emph{impossible}, not unethical, and students
    routinely mislabel them; the distinction is worth insisting on because the
    exit ticket asks for it.

    Section 3 is deliberately the same table shape as Lesson 1.5's. If you still
    have 1.5's notes, put them side by side. Only \emph{Collect} changes, and
    the follow-up blank is the whole point: \emph{Collect --- it assigns.}

    Section 4's reference table is given; the work is the matching table below
    it. Do the first row together. Then read the ``Careful'' sentence at the
    bottom aloud and stop --- \emph{observation} the method versus
    \emph{observational study} the design is the most common error in this
    lesson, and it is worth two full minutes.

    If time runs short, compress section 3 to the follow-up blank alone. Never
    compress section 4; PS.DC.3e appears nowhere else in the unit.
\end{teachernote}

\begin{teachernote}[Group Activity]
    Groups of three, 15--18 minutes, tiers assigned rather than chosen. Read the
    headline box aloud --- it is dense, and it is the whole unit in one
    paragraph: a survey, an experiment, and a new question, all on the same
    $1{,}500$ members.

    Tier R item 1 is the sorting task and item 2 is the reason for it. ``The
    director'' is still half credit in 2(b); ``chance'' or ``the generator'' is
    full. The two divisions have equal denominators, so the arithmetic should
    not slow anyone down.

    Tier A item 2 is the cycle table and the item most groups under-answer ---
    a \emph{Collect} row that says ``she collected data'' has not noticed that
    the assigning happens there. Push for the generator and the two group
    sizes. Item 3 wants two sentences with two different verbs; if a group
    writes ``caused'' in 3(a), that is the item to pull to the whole class.
    Item 4 is the PS.DC.3e item: interviews, because \emph{why} needs follow-up
    questions and only a few people quit.

    Tier E item 1 is the payoff --- arthritis cannot be handed out, so the
    strongest design is unavailable and the claim has to weaken. Item 2's
    competing variable is age, and groups who find it have done exactly what
    Lesson 1.5 taught. Item 3 is three quick matches; it is deliberately cheap,
    so a group that gets there has time to finish it.
\end{teachernote}

\boxguard[22]
\begin{teachernote}[Exit Ticket]
    Five minutes, notes closed, collected at the door. Item 1(a) is a gift and
    exists to make sure nobody leaves without computing something; 1(b) should
    be near-universal after the activity.

    Item 1(c) needs the mechanism, not the label. ``Study B, because it is an
    experiment'' is half credit; full credit says a generator decided the
    groups, so the two groups started out alike.

    Item 2(b) is the one that separates the class. The correct reason is
    \emph{impossible} --- you cannot assign where a family lives. A student who
    answers ``unethical'' has the right verdict from the wrong reason; write
    the distinction on the paper rather than marking it wrong, because the
    verdict matters more and the reason is fixable in one sentence.

    Grade item 3 first and only item 3 if time is short. Full credit names
    interviews or a focus group \emph{and} says what a fixed survey cannot do
    here --- ask a follow-up, or find out what a student means by an answer.
    ``Interviews'' alone is half credit. Students who choose a survey for
    everything are the ones to catch before Lesson 1.8 opens.
\end{teachernote}

\boxguard[26]
\begin{teachernote}[Homework]
    Assign all eight items; about $30$ minutes. Items 1, 4, 5, 6, 7, and 8
    cannot be answered by computing anything --- flag that when assigning,
    because students who treat this as a calculation sheet will do items 2 and
    3 and stop.

    Items 2 and 3 are the arithmetic spine: $64\%$, $40\%$, the $24$-point gap,
    then the pooled $52\%$ and $624$. Both denominators are $150$, so an error
    here is a place-value slip rather than a method problem.

    Item 5's three rows are the sorting task from notes section 2. Row one can
    be assigned --- a library really can set a late fee by chance for one group
    --- and rows two and three cannot, both for the \emph{impossible} reason
    rather than the unethical one. Row three is the subtle one: the treatment
    is in the past, so nobody can go back and assign it.

    Item 6 covers all five methods, one each. The row students miss is the
    self-checkout: it asks what people \emph{do}, which is observation, not a
    survey about what they think they do.

    Item 7 is the closer and the reason this homework reuses the library. The
    $48$-point gap is the biggest number and the weakest evidence, because the
    card holders who signed up were already the heavy readers. Item 8 asks the
    same idea as a writing task --- students must produce the honest headline
    \emph{and} say what would have earned the original one. A student who
    rewrites the headline but skips the second half has done half the item.

    The \textbf{remindbox} hands off to Lesson 1.8, where the class runs its own
    study. Point out that the extension box is the project's first page in
    miniature.
\end{teachernote}
